\title[The energy productivity of neighbourhood form]{The energy productivity of neighbourhood form: everyday access per unit of energy across England}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Gareth} \sur{Simons}}\email{gareth@benchmarkurbanism.com}
\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{TODO department}, \orgname{University College London}, \orgaddress{\city{London}, \country{United Kingdom}}}

\abstract{Neighbourhoods are conventionally assessed by the energy they consume, without reference to what that energy obtains. This paper measures both quantities for 178,353 English Census Output Areas: everyday access (amenities, jobs and people reachable over the road network) against household energy (metered gas and electricity plus National Travel Survey-anchored car travel), together with the ratio between them, an energy productivity for place. On foot, a flat-type neighbourhood reaches about 27 times the amenities of a detached-type one. At each area's own driving range the reachable counts nearly converge, but the detached area closes the gap only through about three times the car-travel energy, so per kilowatt-hour of driving a flat obtains about 3.9 times the access. Total household energy is about 2.1 times higher per dwelling in detached areas, largely reflecting the additional travel that dispersed form requires. The technology measures of national decarbonisation strategy, insulation, heat pumps and electric vehicles, reduce consumption in every neighbourhood when fully deployed, yet close less than a third of the energy gap between forms and none of the access gap. The access deficit of dispersed form is structural, unaffected by the technology pathway, and therefore requires direct measurement and planning.}

\keywords{urban form, accessibility, energy, decarbonisation, compact city, neighbourhood}

%% Introduction (unheaded, per Nature research-article shape).

Energy policy and neighbourhood assessment evaluate places by the energy they consume. Consumption alone, however, records nothing of what the energy obtains: the work, shops, schools and services within a household's reach. Economies are routinely assessed by their energy productivity, output per unit of energy input; no comparable measure exists for neighbourhoods, whose output is the daily activity their residents can reach.

This paper assesses neighbourhoods by access gained per unit of energy spent. The premise is ecological: a system extracts more function from each unit of energy when it is compact and complex, cycling energy through many close exchanges rather than allowing it to dissipate \citep{jacobs1969,jacobs2000}, and compact urban form supports such cycling where dispersed form does not. The object of study is therefore the ratio between two measured quantities, energy spent and access gained. Neither quantity is informative alone: an energy measure classifies a remote dwelling with little within reach as efficient, and an access measure cannot distinguish proximity achieved through built density from equivalent reach achieved through additional driving. Distinguishing these cases requires the ratio.

Prior work treats the two quantities separately, and each strand carries a known measurement hazard. On the building side, compact forms lose less heat: simulation puts the heat-demand contrast between detached and compact archetypes as high as sixfold \citep{rode2014}, while English metered data place detached gas consumption at about twice a flat's \citep{need2024,ehs2024,wyatt2013,buyuklieva2023}. The gap between those figures is largely the energy performance gap: certificate ratings are modelled, over-predict consumption, and over-predict most for the largest dwellings \citep{crawley2019,summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}, so modelled energy inflates any form gradient. On the travel side, density associates with lower car use \citep{newman1989}, but the marginal effect is modest \citep{echenique2012}, and the relationship is better read as one of accessibility than of density as such \citep{ewing2018,ellder2022}; at the metropolitan scale the compact-city case remains contested \citep{gaigne2012,mindali2004}. Cutting across both strands is the functional-unit problem: a low-density energy premium that is large per capita narrows or reverses per square metre \citep{norman2006}, because energy is sub-linear in household size \citep{druckman2008,huebner2017}, so conclusions are sensitive to the choice of denominator.

This separation is reproduced in policy, where the two families of instruments bearing on neighbourhood energy each address one of the quantities. The instruments of buildings and transport decarbonisation act on, and are evaluated by, the consumption of individual assets: fabric standards and retrofit subsidy keyed to the energy certificate, heat-pump deployment targets, and vehicle electrification mandates \citep{ukgovernment2023,ccc2025}. Each instrument is evaluated by the energy it removes from the individual asset, and none is informed by the asset's location: an identical fabric measure yields an identical rating improvement for a dwelling remote from every daily destination and for one adjacent to them. The internal weaknesses of this asset-level accounting are documented, in certificate over-prediction \citep{crawley2019,few2023} and in the prebound gap between rated and actual consumption \citep{sunikka-blank2012}; the structural limitation is external to it. The travel demand generated by a dwelling's location is not represented in the assessment at all, although compact development measurably reduces driving \citep{trb2009,ewing2018}, and although modelling of mobility transitions finds vehicle-technology substitution alone too slow to meet cumulative carbon budgets without demand reduction \citep{winkler2023}.

Planning approaches centred on accessibility address the second quantity without expressing it in energy terms. This line of work, from the critique of automobile dependence \citep{newman1999} through accessibility-based transport instruments \citep{rode2017} to the fifteen-minute city \citep{moreno2021}, targets proximity directly, and mobility-data studies confirm that local amenity supply is the dominant determinant of local travel behaviour \citep{abbiasov2024}. Its metrics, however, are counts and travel times. Because the energy consequence of proximity is not quantified, proximity cannot enter the carbon accounting that allocates decarbonisation effort and cannot be compared with retrofit or electrification on a common unit, so its case is made in terms of quality of life rather than of energy. The two literatures consequently share no common unit of evaluation; the ratio examined here supplies one.

Whether the consumption-centred strategy suffices is, moreover, an empirical question with a testable form: if the energy cost of dispersed form is structural, fixed in floor area and travel distance, technology lowers consumption everywhere without closing the gap between forms, and the access cost is unaffected entirely. England provides a suitable test because its decarbonisation pathway is legislated and quantified \citep{ccc2025}, so the technology scenarios evaluated below correspond to the composition of the national strategy rather than to hypothetical policy bundles.

This paper measures both axes for 178,353 of England's 178,605 Census 2021 Output Areas: network-based access to amenities, jobs and people against metered household energy plus survey-anchored car-travel energy, with the rate between them, modelling energy per dwelling with household size and floor area as freely estimated controls, which resolves the functional-unit ambiguity. The closest antecedent to the rate is the mobility energy productivity metric \citep{hou2019}, a composite planning score weighting opportunity accessibility by modelled mode energy intensities for United States locations; a measured access-per-kilowatt-hour contrast between dwelling forms, built from metered energy at national scale, has to our knowledge no precedent. Because households select their dwellings \citep{cao2009,stevens2017}, the estimand is place-level, the profile of a neighbourhood type conditional on observed confounds, and its exposure to residential self-selection is bounded directly. Figure~\ref{fig:inversion} summarises the central result.

\section*{Results}

Every flat-to-detached contrast below comes from one compositional, no-intercept regression on each Output Area's dwelling-type mix, household-weighted, holding building age, deprivation, tenure and local climate constant (Methods). The contrast is read between idealised pure types, so it states the upper end of each gap; the gap between typical mixed areas is smaller, and both are reported. Confidence intervals are 95\%, clustered on 309 local-authority districts.

\subsection*{The access axis}

Access is the count of opportunities reachable from a neighbourhood over the road network: amenities (six everyday destination types), jobs (each workplace weighted by its job count) and people (resident population), each read at a short walk (1,600\,m, about twenty minutes on foot), at the area's own car catchment, and at a long drive (25,600\,m) (Methods). Access is a property of the location rather than of its residents, so it does not depend on how the household is counted, and it is unaffected by residential self-selection by construction.

The access gap is largest at walking distance and remains open at every distance examined (Table~\ref{tab:axes}, Fig.~\ref{fig:reach}). On foot, a flat-type neighbourhood reaches 27.2$\times$ (95\% CI 19.4--38.1) the amenities, 52.4$\times$ (30.0--91.6) the jobs and 12.5$\times$ (9.4--16.5) the people of a detached-type one. These are pure-type contrasts; between typical areas the on-foot amenity gap is 9.7$\times$ comparing dominant-type medians and 12.2$\times$ comparing areas at least half of one type. At each area's own car catchment the raw counts nearly converge (amenities 1.26$\times$, 1.05--1.51), because a detached area drives further into its larger catchment. At a 25.6\,km drive, where a detached area can reach into denser places, the flat still leads 11.3$\times$ on amenities, 14.4$\times$ on jobs and 11.1$\times$ on people. For context, a flat neighbourhood holds 79 residents per hectare against a detached one's 14, a factor of 5.7.

Figure~\ref{fig:map} maps the two quantities. By energy, dispersed high-consumption form covers most of England's land area, and the compact cities form the low-consumption minority; by walkable access, the distribution reverses, with high access confined almost entirely to those same cities. The corresponding contrast within a single city, between a compact core and a dispersed edge, is shown in the Extended Data.

\subsection*{The energy axis}

Household energy is delivered energy per dwelling per year, in two measured components: the home's metered gas and electricity, and the car travel of its residents. The home component is metered rather than modelled, which avoids the performance gap; the travel component disaggregates measured National Travel Survey mileage across neighbourhoods under exact class constraints (Methods).

A detached neighbourhood spends 2.12$\times$ (95\% CI 2.01--2.24) a flat's total energy per dwelling (Table~\ref{tab:axes}, Fig.~\ref{fig:energy}). The gradient is steepest in travel, 3.07$\times$ (2.81--3.35); the metered home component is 1.60$\times$ (1.45--1.76). The home-energy gap combines three consequences of low density: larger dwellings, larger households, and a more exposed envelope. Holding household size equal moves it to 1.27$\times$ (1.16--1.40); holding floor area equal as well leaves 1.17$\times$ (1.06--1.30), the direct effect of exposed walls and unshared surfaces. On the log scale, size and occupancy account for 66\% (54--85\%) of the home-energy gap, a descriptive covariate adjustment rather than an identified mediation. The estimated elasticity of home energy is 0.54 with respect to floor area and 0.5 with respect to household size, both well below one, which is why no fixed denominator is used: dividing by residents or by floor area would impose an elasticity of one that the data reject.

Flats record fewer domestic gas meters than households (0.81 per household against 0.94 for detached), mostly because all-electric flats heat with electricity, which the total captures; communal heating metered as non-domestic is a genuine, smaller undercount. Restricting to well-measured areas (gas-meter coverage at least 0.9) leaves the home-energy gap at 1.61$\times$, so the measurement issue, if anything, slightly understates it. The same restriction lowers the total gap to 1.74$\times$, a composition effect of the subsample, which excludes the off-gas rural detached areas with the highest travel energy.

\subsection*{The rate: access per kilowatt-hour}

The rate relates the access a neighbourhood obtains to the car-travel energy through which it obtains it: amenities within the area's own car catchment, divided by the area's car-travel energy. The flat-to-detached contrast of the rate is, by construction, the product of the two quantities already reported: at their own catchments the two forms reach a similar count (access advantage 1.26$\times$), but the detached area does so with about three times the car energy (energy saving 3.07$\times$), so per kilowatt-hour the flat obtains $1.26 \times 3.07 \approx 3.9\times$ (95\% CI 3.1--4.8) the access (Fig.~\ref{fig:rate}).

The value depends on how the two components are conditioned, and this dependence is reported. Matching the access side's income-only conditioning on the energy side raises the rate to 5.3$\times$ (4.2--6.7); computing it against an already-electrified travel cost, which removes the dependence between driving distance and catchment size, lowers it to 2.3$\times$ (2.0--2.7); the dominant-type median rate is 3.0$\times$. The headline 3.9$\times$ lies within this range, and the ordering is preserved under every variant considered.

The convergence of catchment counts is the reason the two quantities require joint measurement. A count taken at each area's own driving range shows the access gap as nearly closed; only the accompanying energy identifies the additional driving through which the closure is achieved.

\subsection*{Decarbonisation scenarios and lock-in}

The scenario ladder is built from the principal technology measures of national decarbonisation strategy, with the Balanced Pathway row corresponding to its legislated trajectory \citep{ccc2025}, so the ladder evaluates the composition of the national strategy rather than a hypothetical programme. Recomputing the energy axis under each measure alone and in combination (Methods), every scenario reduces absolute consumption in every neighbourhood, no scenario closes half the energy gap between forms, and none changes the access gap. The three measures act differently. Insulation scales each home's gas by its own certificate-derived fabric headroom, which is larger for detached homes, and closes the most of any single measure: fully deployed it moves the total gap from 2.12$\times$ to 1.83$\times$, 20\% of the log gap. Electrification converts car mileage to electric energy intensity and closes 18\%, to 1.85$\times$. Heat pumps reduce delivered home energy sharply but near-uniformly, which raises the relative weight of the larger travel gap; fully deployed they leave the total gap marginally wider, at 2.16$\times$. On the Climate Change Committee's Balanced Pathway for 2040, with half of homes on heat pumps and three-quarters of cars electric \citep{ccc2025}, the gap stands at 1.89$\times$, one sixth closed. Full deployment of all three levers leaves 1.68$\times$, with 69\% of the log gap surviving; insulation plus full electrification without heat pumps leaves 1.51$\times$, and 55\% (51--58\%) survives.

The access gap is unchanged in every scenario, because none of these technologies alters the distance between dwellings and destinations (Fig.~\ref{fig:scenarios}); the on-foot amenity contrast remains 27$\times$ throughout. The access deficit of dispersed form is fixed in the street layout and the location of destinations, which change over generations rather than over technology-replacement cycles.

\subsection*{Robustness}

Figure~\ref{fig:forest} presents every headline ratio with its clustered confidence interval. Three families of checks apply.

First, residential self-selection. Households who choose detached homes may differ in unmeasured ways that also raise energy use, so the energy gaps are read as place-level profiles rather than household treatment effects. Adding occupational class to the confounds leaves the gaps essentially unchanged. A coefficient-stability bound \citep{oster2019} on a continuous detached-share specification gives $\delta^{*} \approx 1.1$ for total energy, meaning that unobserved sorting would need to be slightly stronger than all measured confounds combined to nullify the gap. The bound indicates survival at the margin rather than insensitivity: at $\delta = 1$ the adjusted gap is 1.03$\times$, and the home-energy component is weaker still ($\delta^{*} \approx 0.3$). For this reason the argument rests on access and the rate, which are properties of location and unaffected by sorting by construction; the energy gap enters as the denominator of the rate rather than as an independent causal claim.

Second, the travel construction. Because each rural-urban class total is anchored exactly to the measured survey figure, the two allocator assumptions only redistribute miles within a class. The commute-distance elasticity has little effect: varying it from 0.15 to 0.45 moves the travel contrast between 2.74$\times$ and 2.98$\times$ on dominant-type medians. The ownership elasticity is the more consequential assumption and is swept to its limiting value: the compositional travel gap is 3.07$\times$ under proportional allocation, 2.93$\times$ at an elasticity of 0.8, 2.80$\times$ at 0.6, and 2.50$\times$ (2.35--2.65) at zero, where the ownership signal is removed and only the measured between-class mileage gradient and household size remain. Under any within-class allocation, a detached neighbourhood spends at least two and a half times a flat's car-travel energy.

Third, measurement and zonation. The home-energy gap holds under meter-to-household denominator restrictions (1.55--1.61$\times$) and winsorisation (1.59$\times$). Re-aggregating the whole frame to the two coarser census geographies and re-fitting the same model, the total gap holds at 2.12$\times$, 1.88$\times$ and 1.72$\times$ at Output Area, LSOA and MSOA scale, with the support-respecting median companion at 1.74$\times$, 1.59$\times$ and 1.47$\times$, so the result is not an artefact of the zonation.

\section*{Discussion}

The component results reproduce the literature where they overlap it, which locates the contribution in the combination rather than in any single component. The metered home-energy gap of 1.60$\times$ agrees with national metered sources \citep{need2024,ehs2024} and dwelling-level regressions \citep{wyatt2013,buyuklieva2023}, and lies well below simulation contrasts \citep{rode2014}, a difference attributable to the performance gap \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}. The dependence of the conclusion on the functional unit reproduces lifecycle results \citep{norman2006}, and the sub-unit household-size elasticity reproduces the downsizing literature \citep{huebner2017,druckman2008}. The travel contrast agrees in direction with the urban-form literature but exceeds marginal-effect estimates \citep{echenique2012} because it is a different estimand: a cross-sectional contrast between the extremes of the form distribution, read against the observed rural-urban mileage gradient. The access measurement operationalises the accessibility interpretation of the density-travel relationship \citep{ewing2018,ellder2022}. The nearest relative of the rate, the mobility energy productivity metric \citep{hou2019}, is a prospective planning score built on modelled travel times and mode energy intensities; the rate here is an empirical national contrast on measured energy, read between dwelling forms and evaluated under decarbonisation scenarios. The household-scale finding does not settle the metropolitan general-equilibrium question, in which relocation responses can offset densification gains \citep{gaigne2012}.

The scenario results state the mechanism precisely. Uniform technology scales energy down without changing the structural quantities: insulation lowers loss per square metre but not the floor area, and electrification lowers energy per mile but not the mileage. Between 55\% and 69\% of the energy gap survives full deployment, and heat pumps, which reduce delivered home energy the most, leave the form gap marginally wider because the remainder is dominated by travel. The access deficit is unchanged in every scenario. This is lock-in in the strict sense \citep{unruh2000,seto2016}: the quantities that set the gap are fixed in built form and street layout for generations. The scenario results also indicate what each form of assessment registers. Evaluated on consumption, the national programme succeeds, because absolute energy falls in every neighbourhood; evaluated on the rate, the same programme leaves the relative position of places, and the access deficit underlying it, essentially unchanged. An assessment framework restricted to consumption therefore provides no indication of the remaining problem.

Whether a detached neighbourhood is more energy-intensive at all depends on the functional unit, and the unit is a modelling choice rather than a presentational one. Per dwelling and per capita it consumes more; per square metre the gap nearly closes; per person it narrows because detached homes hold larger households. Since the household-size elasticity of home energy is about 0.5, per-person normalisation imposes an elasticity of one that the data reject, and thereby credits detached homes for housing the larger households that select into them. The specification adopted here, energy per dwelling with household size and floor area as freely estimated controls, allows the data to determine the scaling and keeps the selection observable.

For policy, the results identify two asymmetries in the current instrument set. First, the instruments address the smaller of the two energy gaps. The asset-level measures, certificates, fabric subsidy and boiler replacement, operate on home energy, where the form gap is 1.60$\times$ and falls to 1.17$\times$ like for like; the travel gap, 3.07$\times$ and robust to every allocation assumption down to a measured floor of 2.50$\times$, has no asset-level instrument, because it is generated by the neighbourhood's location rather than by any asset a household owns. Second, the largest available energy measure is not scored by any existing instrument. A compact location provides 3.9$\times$ the access per kilowatt-hour of driving, a larger differential than any fabric measure in the scenario ladder delivers, yet no certificate, subsidy or planning metric records it, so the one measure that improves both quantities is absent from the accounting that allocates decarbonisation effort. A neighbourhood rating restricted to consumption compounds the problem by rewarding low-density form for its low per-capita heat while ignoring its travel and access penalties.

A two-axis measure addresses both asymmetries: it expresses proximity, which retrofit and electrification cannot supply, in the same unit as the measures with which it competes for resources. Decarbonising buildings and vehicles is necessary but not sufficient, and access has to be a planned outcome, scored alongside energy. We refer to this measure, the two quantities and the ratio between them, as the neighbourhood energy performance index (NEPI): a neighbourhood scored as an energy performance certificate scores a building, with one addition. Alongside the current score, a technology-potential score can be computed under full deployment, and the distance between the two, substantial for energy and nil for access, expresses the lock-in for each neighbourhood individually. Two caveats apply. Energy saved through efficiency is partly re-spent, so dwelling-level gains need not scale to economy-wide reductions. And access is capitalised into land and rent, so the advantages of compact form are not shared evenly; an access score should be read alongside distributional evidence rather than as a welfare conclusion.

Five limitations qualify the result. The estimand is place-level, and the definitive self-selection test, differencing out fixed preferences with movers in panel microdata, is out of scope. The analysis is ecological, at area rather than household level, with the usual inferential cautions \citep{robinson1950,greenland2001}. The insulation lever assumes the certificate potential-to-current ratio captures achievable fabric improvement. The access counts treat amenity location as exogenous, though amenities partly follow demand; the near-convergence of counts at each area's own catchment is consistent with the compact advantage being one of proximity rather than supply, and the access figures describe what residents can currently reach. And the analysis is England-only, pending a harmonised Welsh deprivation measure.

Assessed by access gained per unit of energy spent, compact form is the more efficient, and the difference is structural: it is fixed in built form and street layout until places are rebuilt, and it is not altered by any technology on the current deployment pathway.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Unit of analysis}

The unit is the 2021 Census Output Area, the smallest published census geography, about 125 households and 300 residents. Of England's 178,605 Output Areas, the 178,353 with usable metered energy are analysed. Wales is excluded because the English Index of Multiple Deprivation, used as a control, has no directly comparable Welsh equivalent. All inputs are open data, processed to the Output Area on the British National Grid (EPSG:27700): Census 2021 topic tables (population, household size, dwelling-type shares, tenure, occupational class, car ownership, commute distance, workplace jobs); DESNZ sub-national gas and electricity statistics; domestic Energy Performance Certificates (floor area, build year, current and potential intensity); OS Open Roads; amenity registers (Food Standards Agency, Get Information about Schools, NHS Organisation Data Service, OS Open Greenspace); the Indices of Deprivation 2025; DVLA vehicle licensing; National Travel Survey table NTS9904; and HadUK-Grid 1991--2020 heating-degree-days.

\subsection*{The energy axis}

Energy is delivered energy in kilowatt-hours per dwelling per year, not primary energy or carbon. The home component is metered: postcode-level gas and electricity, each a meter count and a mean per meter, are aggregated to the Output Area by meter-weighted means, summed, and divided by census households. Gas is weather-corrected; both series cover domestic meters only. Metered energy is used because certificate ratings over-predict consumption, most for large detached dwellings, and would inflate the form gradient with model error \citep{crawley2019,summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}.

The travel component is the total car-travel energy of a location's residents, not the commute alone (about one sixth of car mileage). It is estimated by constrained disaggregation. NTS9904 gives measured car-driver miles per person by 2021 rural-urban class of residence, from about 2,500 (urban) to 5,200 (rural). Each class total is distributed across its Output Areas by an allocator combining cars per person (elasticity 1, the proportional headline) and commute distance (elasticity 0.30), constrained so each class's population-weighted mean reproduces the survey figure exactly; only the within-class distribution is estimated, and both elasticities are swept in the sensitivity analysis. Miles are converted at the local fleet's energy per mile, 0.93 (internal combustion) and 0.32 (battery electric) kWh per mile, blended by the DVLA battery-electric share. Metered domestic electricity already contains home electric-vehicle charging, so the travel figure double-counts that overlap; at the current battery-electric fleet share (2--3\%) the effect is negligible.

\subsection*{The access axis}

Access is the count of opportunities reachable from an Output Area over the road network. The England network (OS Open Roads, about 3.6 million junctions) is built once into a routable graph (cityseer \citep{simons2023}), and counts are accumulated outward from each Output Area's nearest node at every distance band from 1,600\,m to 25,600\,m. Amenities are six everyday destination types: general practitioners and pharmacies (NHS), schools (GIAS), food outlets and food shops (FSA), and public parks and greenspace (Ordnance Survey). Jobs are workplace jobs, each workplace weighted by its job count; people are resident population. Each is read at 1,600\,m, at the area's own car catchment, and at 25,600\,m.

\subsection*{The rate}

Per Output Area the rate is the amenity count within the area's own car catchment, divided by its car-travel energy. The catchment is the area's NTS car-driver distance per person divided by 370 trips per person per year, a constant national trip rate, with the resulting one-way distance used as network reach and bounded to [1,600\,m, 25,600\,m]. The flat-to-detached contrast of the rate is by construction the product of the access advantage (catchment amenities, flat:detached) and the energy saving (car-travel energy, detached:flat); it is reconstructable from Tables~\ref{tab:axes} and~\ref{tab:axes}, not estimated as a separate model. The two terms are deliberately not independent: the catchment radius and the travel energy are both functions of how far residents drive, which is the intended reading. The conditioning variants reported in the Results bound the rate's dependence on that construction.

\subsection*{The compositional model}

All contrasts come from one compositional, no-intercept regression on each Output Area's dwelling-type shares (flat, terraced, semi-detached, detached, residual) as fractions summing to one. Without an intercept each coefficient is the modelled outcome of a neighbourhood composed entirely of one type, and the ratio is $\exp(b_{\mathrm{detached}} - b_{\mathrm{flat}})$, invariant to the uncentred confounds. Regressions are household-weighted. The estimate is read at pure-composition vertices, which few areas approach, so each ratio states the contrast between idealised extremes; support-respecting median companions are reported alongside.

Energy enters in logarithms per dwelling, so fitted ratios are ratios of conditional geometric means. Household size and floor area are covariates with freely estimated coefficients, never denominators. Four confounds are held throughout: building age, deprivation (the overall Index of Multiple Deprivation and its income domain), tenure (social- and private-rented shares), and climate (heating-degree-days). Access counts are non-negative with frequent zeros and are fitted with a Poisson log link; with households as analytic weights the estimator is quasi-Poisson, using point estimates with a robust sandwich covariance. The access regression holds the income domain but not density, which is the mechanism under study, and not the overall deprivation index, whose barriers and living-environment sub-domains are themselves access measures.

Standard errors are clustered on the 309 local-authority districts, with $t(G-1)$ critical values; every reported ratio carries a 95\% interval on this basis. Composite quantities (the rate, the surviving-gap share, the mediated fraction) carry cluster-bootstrap intervals with 999 replications, resampling whole districts.

\subsection*{Decarbonisation scenarios}

The energy axis is recomputed under technology levers and the gap re-estimated. Insulation scales each home's metered gas by its certificate fabric-improvement ratio (modelled potential over current intensity, median 0.5; both terms modelled, so the performance gap cancels); electricity is unchanged. Heat pumps deliver the gas heat as electricity at the delivered-energy factor of boiler efficiency over a seasonal coefficient of performance ($0.90/2.8 = 0.32$); a sensitivity varies the coefficient between 2.4 and 3.2. Electrification recomputes car energy at electric fleet intensity with mileage unchanged. Both heat levers act on metered gas only, so electrically heated homes, commoner among flats, receive no retrofit lever; the direction is conservative for the surviving-gap shares. Partial uptake blends transformed and untransformed energy linearly; the Balanced Pathway scenario uses the Climate Change Committee's 2040 stock shares \citep{ccc2025}. Every scenario is fitted on one common complete-case sample (178,341 areas with a valid fabric ratio). Access is unchanged in every scenario by construction.

\subsection*{Robustness and the estimand}

The estimand is place-level: the energy and access profile of a neighbourhood type conditional on observed confounds, not a household treatment effect. Three checks bound selection on unobservables. Access depends on location and is immune by construction. Occupational class (higher managerial and professional share) is added to the confounds and moves the gaps by essentially nothing. A coefficient-stability bound \citep{oster2019} is computed on a continuous detached-share gradient in an intercept specification (the compositional model has no centred $R^2$), using the coefficient-based approximation with $R_{\max} = \min(1.3\tilde{R}, 1)$; the Results report $\delta^{*}$ together with the $\delta = 1$ bias-adjusted gap. The zonation check re-aggregates all variables household-weighted to LSOA and MSOA geographies and re-fits the identical model.
