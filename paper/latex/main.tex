\abstract{Energy and decarbonisation policy evaluates homes and vehicles directly, yet together these assets constitute a neighbourhood form that sets limits on what the policies can achieve. Planning has long quantified proximity, but conventionally in counts and travel times, not in energy. This paper joins the two evaluations: it quantifies the everyday access a neighbourhood obtains per unit of energy spent, an energy productivity for places analogous to that of economies. Together they reveal a structural lock-in that outlasts the piecemeal gains of asset replacement and embeds the penalty of dispersed form for generations. Across \nepisampleN{} Census 2021 Output Areas we measure network access to amenities, jobs and people, total energy from metered gas and electricity plus survey-anchored car travel, and the rate between them. Comparing idealised pure dwelling types, a flat neighbourhood reaches about \nepiwalkAmenRound{} times the amenities of a detached one on foot. Matching that count costs the detached neighbourhood \nepitravelGap{} times the car-travel energy, and \nepitotalGapRound{} times the total energy per dwelling once home energy is included. Per unit of energy spent, the flat obtains \nepirate{} times the access. Technology reduces consumption in compact and dispersed neighbourhoods alike, but closes neither gap. The most favourable case for the deployment pathway insulates every home to its certificate potential and electrifies every car; even then a dispersed neighbourhood spends \nepilockinGap{} times the energy of a compact one. Heat pumps widen the energy gap by cutting home consumption and leaving the larger travel gap dominant. The access gap remains unchanged in all scenarios, because no measure brings a destination closer. We therefore argue that decarbonisation strategy should place greater emphasis on compact urban form.}

\keywords{urban form, accessibility, energy, decarbonisation, compact city, neighbourhood}

Energy and decarbonisation policy evaluates homes and vehicles directly, by the energy they consume and the carbon they emit, and the remedies follow the metric: insulation, heat pumps and electric vehicles \citep{ukgovernment2023,ccc2025}. Planning and urbanism evaluate the neighbourhoods these assets constitute by walkability and accessibility, and prescribe mixed use, higher density and the fifteen-minute city \citep{moreno2021}. Both kinds of consumption, however, are set substantially by the same variable. The form of a place determines heat demand through shared walls and dwelling size, and travel demand through the distance between homes and daily destinations; dispersed form raises both together, and its car-travel energy is spent obtaining the access that compact form provides through proximity. We argue that the two quantities carry more information combined than either carries in isolation, as a ratio of access gained to energy spent (hereafter the rate). The rate asks of any neighbourhood what its energy obtains, a question neither field ordinarily takes as its point of departure, and states the answer as an energy productivity for places, analogous to the energy productivity of an economy \citep{patterson1996}. The rate exposes a structural lock-in in current policy: the instruments on the current deployment pathway act on the energy axis, so the access deficit of dispersed form goes unaddressed. Even in the best case for technology, with every home insulated to its certificate potential and every car electric, dispersed form still spends in the region of \nepilockinGap$\times$ the energy of compact form.

The premise comes from Jane Jacobs's idea of energy in the conduit \citep{jacobs2000}: complexity, compactness and diversity allow a system to make more effective use of the energy that passes through it, in a literal sense. Jacobs illustrated the idea with a rainforest and a desert: the rainforest folds a unit of incoming energy through many organisms and close exchanges, extracting use from it repeatedly before it dissipates; the desert, for an equivalent amount of energy, does less, because the energy streams through the system and is lost. She applied the principle to economies \citep{jacobs1969,jacobs2000}, and ideas of the same kind run through her urban thinking: urban density, functional diversity and close exchange \citep{jacobs1961}. Applied to settlements, the principle implies that energy productivity should rise with urban compactness. The urban scaling literature gives the same principle quantitative form: as cities grow, incomes, productivity and innovation rise faster than population does, while infrastructure and service delivery require less per person and quality of life improves \citep{bettencourt2007,glaeser2011}. This paper puts the principle to a direct test. It measures energy spent and access gained across the morphological gradient of England's neighbourhoods, from flats through terraces and semi-detached houses to detached houses, with each area's dwelling-type composition standing for its compactness. The test asks whether the rate rises as form grows more compact.

The present study builds on two strands of previous work. The first, in transport and accessibility research, is concerned with reaching destinations, and developed the idea of valuing access by what it costs to obtain. Hou and colleagues, seeking a measure of the quality of mobility a United States location affords, constructed the mobility energy productivity metric \citep{hou2019}. The metric counts the opportunities reachable from a place, weights them by the time, money and energy required to reach them, and aggregates over driving, transit and walking, with travel times and mode energy intensities supplied by transport models. Kinigadner and colleagues made carbon the accessibility currency: carbon-based accessibility counts the destinations reachable within an emission budget rather than a time budget, and was developed to evaluate transport interventions and workplace locations for low-carbon planning \citep{kinigadner2020}. Cui and Levinson extended the cost of reaching destinations to its full social cost: full cost accessibility monetises time, money, crash risk and emissions into one generalised cost, and computes accessibility against it \citep{cui2018}. All three are planning tools by design: their inputs are modelled travel times, mode shares and intensity factors, and their outputs are prospective scores for comparing locations and proposals rather than records of what a neighbourhood spends.

The second strand, in energy research, is concerned with what households consume and how that consumption is distributed. Chatterton and colleagues joined the two measured records available at national scale, metered domestic gas and electricity and car energy derived from odometer readings, into a map of direct household energy across the 34,753 Lower-layer Super Output Areas of England and Wales, census neighbourhoods of about 1,600 residents \citep{chatterton2016}. Their purpose was distributional, a new perspective on energy justice: a minority of areas consume far more than the rest, and they argued that carbon policy should attend to these high consumers and not only to the energy poor. They also raised, in terms of need, choice and lock-in, the question this paper takes up: whether high consumption reflects structural constraint rather than choice, with housing type and poor accessibility among the candidate causes. Answering that question requires the access axis, which their record does not contain.

Whether decarbonising assets also equalises places is the question these strands leave open, and answering it imposes four requirements at once. The evaluation needs both axes, or the access half of the question is invisible; measured energy, or the answer inherits model bias; the whole stock, or it cannot speak to a national strategy; and form as the exposure, because form is the lever the strategy omits. The first strand supplies the evaluation but as a prospective score; the second supplies measurement and national coverage but for energy alone. Neither holds all four requirements, so the question has remained open. To our knowledge the present study is the first to hold them together: the rate computed from measured consumption for every English neighbourhood, read across the morphological gradient from flats to detached houses, and its energy axis recomputed under the decarbonisation scenarios of the national strategy.

The separation is reproduced in policy, where the two families of instruments bearing on neighbourhood energy each address one of the quantities. The instruments of buildings and transport decarbonisation act on the consumption of individual assets and are evaluated by it: fabric standards and retrofit subsidy keyed to the energy certificate, heat-pump deployment targets, and vehicle electrification mandates \citep{ukgovernment2023,ccc2025}. These instruments are not typically informed by the asset's location: an identical fabric measure yields an identical rating improvement for a dwelling remote from all daily destinations and for one adjacent to them. The internal weaknesses of this asset-level accounting are documented, in certificate over-prediction \citep{summerfield2019,few2023} and the prebound gap between rated and actual consumption \citep{sunikka-blank2012}. The limitation examined here is external to that accounting: the travel demand generated by a dwelling's location is not represented in the assessment. Compact development measurably reduces driving \citep{trb2009,ewing2017,ewing2018}, and modelling of mobility transitions finds vehicle-technology substitution alone too slow to meet cumulative carbon budgets without demand reduction \citep{winkler2023}. Planning approaches centred on accessibility address the second quantity, typically without expressing it in energy terms. This line of work, from the critique of automobile dependence \citep{newman1999} through accessibility-based transport instruments \citep{rode2017} to the fifteen-minute city \citep{moreno2021}, targets proximity directly. A large mobility-data study finds that local amenity supply is the primary driver of local trip-making \citep{abbiasov2024}, and cities with higher walking-based proximity emit measurably less mobility carbon per capita \citep{marzolla2025}. Its metrics, however, are chiefly counts and travel times, and while the energy consequence of proximity remains unquantified, proximity cannot enter the carbon accounting that allocates decarbonisation effort or be compared with retrofit or electrification. The two literatures consequently share no established common unit of evaluation, which the rate supplies.

The lock-in claim has a testable form: if the energy cost of dispersed form is structural, fixed in floor area and travel distance, then technology lowers consumption everywhere without closing the gap between forms, and the access deficit is unaffected. This paper runs that test by measuring both axes for \nepisampleN{} of England's 178,605 Census 2021 Output Areas: network-based access to amenities, jobs and people against metered home energy plus survey-anchored car-travel energy, with the rate between them. England provides a suitable setting because its carbon budgets are legislated and the advised pathway to them is quantified \citep{ccc2025}, so the technology scenarios evaluated below correspond to the composition of the national strategy rather than to hypothetical policy bundles.

Three measurement hazards, documented in the evidence on each side, shape the design. On the building side, compact morphologies lose less heat, but the size of the contrast depends on how energy is measured: simulation places the heat-demand gap between detached and compact archetypes at up to sixfold \citep{rode2014}, while English national consumption statistics and metered analyses place detached gas consumption at about twice that of a flat \citep{need2024,ehs2024,wyatt2013}. The difference is largely the energy performance gap: certificate ratings are modelled and noisily assessed \citep{crawley2019}, over-predict consumption, and over-predict most for the largest dwellings \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}, so modelled energy inflates any form gradient. Home energy is therefore metered, keeping this model error out of the gradient. On the travel side, density is associated with lower car use \citep{newman1989}, but the marginal effect is modest \citep{ewing2017,echenique2012}, the relationship is better read as one of accessibility than of density as such \citep{ewing2018,ellder2022}, and at the metropolitan scale the compact-city case remains contested \citep{gaigne2012,mindali2004}. Travel energy is anchored to measured national mileage, with the allocation assumptions swept in the robustness analysis, and access is measured over the road network rather than proxied by density. The third hazard is the functional unit: a low-density energy premium that is large per capita narrows or reverses per square metre \citep{norman2006}, because energy is sub-linear in household size \citep{druckman2008,huebner2017}. Energy accordingly enters per dwelling, with household size and floor area as freely estimated controls. Because households select their dwellings \citep{cao2009,stevens2017}, the estimand is place-level: the profile of a neighbourhood type conditional on observed confounds. Its exposure to residential self-selection is bounded directly. Figure~\ref{fig:inversion} summarises the central result.

\section*{Results}

Every flat-to-detached contrast below derives from one compositional, no-intercept regression on each Output Area's dwelling-type mix, household-weighted, holding building age, deprivation, tenure and local climate constant (Methods). The contrast is read between idealised pure types, so it states the upper end of each gap; the gap between typical mixed areas is smaller, and both figures are reported. Confidence intervals are 95\%, clustered on \nepiclusters{} local-authority districts. The argument rests on access and the rate; the energy gap enters through the rate's denominator rather than as an independent causal claim.

\subsection*{The access axis}

Access is the count of opportunities reachable from an area over the road network: amenities (six everyday destination types), jobs (each workplace weighted by its job count) and people (resident population). Each count is read at a short walk (1,600\,m, about twenty minutes on foot), at the area's own car catchment and at a long drive (25,600\,m) (Methods). Access is a property of the location rather than of its residents, so it does not depend on how the household is counted, and it is, by construction, unaffected by residential self-selection.

The access gap is largest at walking distance and remains open at every distance examined (Table~\ref{tab:axes}, Fig.~\ref{fig:reach}). On foot, a flat-type neighbourhood reaches \nepiwalkAmen$\times$ (95\% CI \nepiwalkAmenCI) the amenities, \nepiwalkJobs$\times$ (\nepiwalkJobsCI) the jobs and \nepiwalkPeople$\times$ (\nepiwalkPeopleCI) the people of a detached-type one. Between typical areas the on-foot amenity gap is \nepiwalkAmenDomMed$\times$ comparing dominant-type medians and \nepiwalkAmenSupport$\times$ comparing areas that are at least half of one type. Per-service counts at walking distance are given in Extended Data Fig.~2. At each area's own car catchment the raw counts nearly converge (amenities \nepicatchAmen$\times$, \nepicatchAmenCI), because a detached area's residents drive further, into a larger catchment. At a 25.6\,km drive, where a detached area's residents can reach denser places, the flat-type area still reaches \nepidriveAmen$\times$ the amenities, \nepidriveJobs$\times$ the jobs and \nepidrivePeople$\times$ the people. For context, a flat-type neighbourhood holds \nepipopDensityFlat{} residents per hectare, compared with \nepipopDensityDet{} in a detached-type one, a factor of \nepipopDensityRatio{} (dominant-type medians). Public-transport access follows the same gradient: a flat-type neighbourhood has \nepitransitAdv$\times$ (\nepitransitAdvCI) the bus and rail stops within 1,600\,m (straight-line count; Methods). Stops are a means of reach rather than destinations, so this gradient is not credited in the access counts, and the public-transport travel that accompanies it is not charged to the energy axis either (Extended Data Table~5). The deficit at walking distance therefore closes only by driving.

\subsection*{The energy axis}

The energy axis measures the cost side: the driving through which the access deficit is closed, together with the home consumption that dispersed form raises alongside it. Total energy is delivered energy per dwelling per year, in two measured components: the home's metered gas and electricity, and the car-travel energy of its residents. The home component is metered rather than modelled, which avoids the performance gap; the travel component is estimated by disaggregating measured National Travel Survey mileage across areas under exact class constraints (Methods).

Total energy per dwelling in a detached neighbourhood is \nepitotalGap$\times$ (95\% CI \nepitotalGapCI) that of a flat-type one, and \nepifamNowGap$\times$ at equal household size (Table~\ref{tab:axes}). The gradient is steepest in travel, \nepitravelGap$\times$ (\nepitravelGapCI); the metered home component is \nepiheatGap$\times$ (\nepiheatGapCI). The home-energy gap combines three consequences of low density: larger dwellings, larger households, and a more exposed envelope. Holding household size equal moves it to \nepiheatFamGap$\times$ (\nepiheatFamGapCI); holding floor area equal as well leaves \nepiheatSizeGap$\times$ (\nepiheatSizeGapCI), the direct effect of exposed walls and unshared surfaces. On the log scale, size and occupancy account for \nepimedShare\% (\nepimedShareCI\%) of the home-energy gap; this is a descriptive covariate adjustment rather than an identified mediation. The bivariate elasticity of home energy with respect to floor area is \nepifloorElast, and the household-size elasticity estimated within the model is \nepihhElast. Both are well below one, so no fixed denominator is used: dividing by residents or by floor area would impose an elasticity of one that the data reject.

Both measured axes can now be placed in space. Figure~\ref{fig:map} maps them side by side. By energy, dispersed high-consumption form covers most of England's land area and the compact cities form a low-consumption minority. By walkable access, the pattern reverses: high access is confined almost entirely to those same cities. The corresponding contrast within a single city, between a compact core and a dispersed edge, is shown in Extended Data Fig.~3, and the joint distribution over all individual areas in Extended Data Fig.~1.

\subsection*{The rate: access per kilowatt-hour}

The near-convergence of catchment counts is the reason the two axes require joint measurement: a count taken at each area's own car catchment shows the access gap as nearly closed, and the energy identifies the additional driving through which the closure is achieved. The rate joins the two quantities: it is the amenity count within the area's own car catchment divided by the area's car-travel energy, and car energy is the denominator because it is the energy through which access beyond walking distance is obtained. At their own catchments the two forms reach a similar count (access advantage \nepicatchAmen$\times$), but the detached area does so with about three times the car energy (energy saving \nepitravelGap$\times$). Per kilowatt-hour, the flat-type area therefore obtains $\nepicatchAmen \times \nepitravelGap \approx \nepirate\times$ (95\% CI \nepirateCI) the access (Fig.~\ref{fig:rate}).

The value depends on how the two components are conditioned, and this dependence is reported. Matching the access side's income-only conditioning on the energy side raises the rate to \nepirateHarm$\times$ (\nepirateHarmCI). Computing the rate against already-electrified car-travel energy, which removes the dependence between driving distance and catchment size, lowers it to \nepirateCirc$\times$ (\nepirateCircCI); the dominant-type median rate is \nepirateDomMedian$\times$. The headline \nepirate$\times$ lies within this range, and the ordering is preserved under every variant considered.

\subsection*{Decarbonisation scenarios and lock-in}

The rate can change only if technology moves one of its two axes, and the technologies on the national pathway all act on the energy axis. The scenario ladder recomputes that axis under the principal technology measures of the strategy, alone and in combination (Methods); the access axis is unchanged in every scenario, because none of these technologies alters the distance between dwellings and destinations, and the on-foot amenity contrast remains \nepiwalkAmenRound$\times$ throughout (Fig.~\ref{fig:scenarios}). Every scenario reduces absolute consumption in every neighbourhood, but the three measures act differently on the gap between forms. Insulation scales each home's gas by its own certificate-derived fabric headroom, which is larger for detached homes, and closes more of the gap than any other single measure: fully deployed, it moves the total gap from \nepiscenarioBase$\times$ to \nepifabricGap$\times$, closing \nepifabricClosed\% of the log gap. Electrification recomputes car mileage at electric energy intensity and closes \nepievClosed\% of the log gap, leaving \nepievGap$\times$. Heat pumps reduce delivered home energy sharply but near-uniformly, which raises the relative weight of the larger travel gap; fully deployed they leave the total gap marginally wider, at \nepihpGap$\times$.

On the Climate Change Committee's Balanced Pathway for 2040, with half of homes on heat pumps and three-quarters of cars electric \citep{ccc2025}, the gap is \nepicccGap$\times$, with \nepicccClosed\% closed. This row corresponds to the pathway advised for the legislated carbon budgets, so the ladder evaluates the composition of the national strategy rather than a hypothetical programme. Full deployment of all three measures leaves the total gap at \nepifullGap$\times$, with \nepifullSurvives\% of the log gap surviving. Confidence intervals for the ladder scenarios are given in Extended Data Table~2.

The headline lock-in bound is read at fully deployed insulation plus vehicle electrification, without heat pumps: heat pumps switch the fuel of delivered heat rather than reduce the demand for it and thereby widen the gap, so excluding them grants the technology pathway its most favourable case. Under that bound the total gap is \nepilockinGap$\times$ (\nepilockinGapCI), with \nepisurvivesShare\% (\nepisurvivesShareCI\%) of the log gap surviving; at equal household size the same measures move \nepifamNowGap$\times$ to \nepifamOptGap$\times$. The access deficit of dispersed form is untouched by any scenario, because it is fixed in the street layout and the location of destinations, which change over generations rather than over technology-replacement cycles.

\subsection*{Robustness}

Figure~\ref{fig:forest} presents every headline ratio with its clustered confidence interval, and the estimates are examined against three threats to validity.

The first concerns residential self-selection: households who choose detached homes may differ in unmeasured ways that also raise energy use, so the energy gaps are read as place-level profiles rather than household treatment effects. Adding occupational class to the confounds leaves the gaps essentially unchanged. A coefficient-stability bound \citep{oster2019} on a continuous detached-share specification gives $\delta^{*} \approx \nepiosterDeltaTotal$ for total energy, indicating that unobserved sorting would need to be slightly stronger than all measured confounds combined to nullify the gap. The bound indicates survival at the margin rather than insensitivity: at $\delta = 1$ the adjusted gap is \nepiosterBetaAdjTotal$\times$, and the home-energy component is weaker still ($\delta^{*} \approx \nepiosterDeltaHeat$; Extended Data Table~3). This is why the claim hierarchy at the head of the Results places access and the rate first.

The second concerns the travel construction: because each rural-urban class total is anchored exactly to the measured survey figure, the two allocator assumptions only redistribute miles within a class. The commute-distance elasticity has little effect: varying it from 0.15 to 0.45 varies the travel contrast between \nepicommuteSweepLo$\times$ and \nepicommuteSweepHi$\times$ on dominant-type medians. The ownership elasticity is the more consequential assumption and is swept to its limiting value: the compositional travel gap is \nepitravelGap$\times$ under proportional allocation, \nepialphaEightGap$\times$ at an elasticity of 0.8, \nepialphaSixGap$\times$ at 0.6, and \nepialphaZeroGap$\times$ (\nepialphaZeroGapCI) at zero. At zero the ownership signal is removed; the between-class mileage gradient, the commute-distance term and household size remain. Under any ownership allocation the travel contrast remains at or above about two and a half times (95\% CI at the zero-elasticity limit \nepialphaZeroGapCI). At equal household size the travel gap is \nepitravelFamGap$\times$ (\nepitravelFamGapCI), so, unlike the home-energy gap, it is not primarily a household-size effect. The axis counts car travel only, and adding public transport on the same construction, its mileage taken from the same survey table and disaggregated by the same constrained method, leaves the total gap at \nepiptAssumedTotal$\times$ (\nepiptAssumedTotalCI) at an assumed \nepiptIntensityAssumed$\times$ the car intensity per passenger-mile, and at \nepiptCeilingTotal$\times$ (\nepiptCeilingTotalCI) when every passenger-mile is charged at the car intensity itself (Extended Data Table~5).

The third concerns measurement and zonation: flat-type areas have fewer domestic gas meters than households (\nepimeterFlat{} per household against \nepimeterDet{} for detached), largely because all-electric flats heat with electricity, which the total captures; communal heating metered as non-domestic is a genuine but smaller undercount. The home-energy gap holds under the corresponding denominator restrictions: restricting the sample to areas with gas-meter coverage of at least 0.9 leaves it at \nepigasCovHeat$\times$, the electricity-meter restriction leaves \nepielecHeat$\times$, and winsorisation leaves \nepiwinsorHeat$\times$, so the measurement issue, to the extent that it biases the estimate, slightly understates the gap. The gas-coverage restriction lowers the total gap to \nepigasCovTotal$\times$, a composition effect of the subsample, which excludes the off-gas rural detached areas with the highest travel energy. Re-aggregating all variables to the two coarser census geographies and re-fitting the same model leaves the total gap at \nepimaupTotalOa$\times$, \nepimaupTotalLsoa$\times$ and \nepimaupTotalMsoa$\times$ at Output Area, LSOA and MSOA scale. The support-respecting median counterpart is \nepimaupMedOa$\times$, \nepimaupMedLsoa$\times$ and \nepimaupMedMsoa$\times$, so the result is not an artefact of the zonation. The home-energy vertex contrast attenuates towards parity at MSOA scale, where no unit approaches a pure composition, while the total gap, on which the argument rests, holds at every scale (Extended Data Table~4).

\section*{Discussion}

The component results agree with the published estimates where they overlap; the contribution therefore lies in the combination rather than in any single component. The metered home-energy gap of \nepiheatGap$\times$ agrees with national consumption statistics \citep{need2024,ehs2024} and dwelling-level metered regressions \citep{wyatt2013}, and lies well below simulation contrasts \citep{rode2014}, a difference attributable to the performance gap \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}. The dependence of the conclusion on the functional unit reproduces lifecycle results \citep{norman2006}, and the below-one household-size elasticity is consistent with the downsizing and household-composition literature \citep{huebner2017,druckman2008}. The travel contrast agrees in direction with the urban-form literature but exceeds marginal-effect estimates \citep{echenique2012} because it is a different estimand: a cross-sectional contrast between the extremes of the form distribution, read against the observed rural-urban mileage gradient. The access measurement operationalises the accessibility interpretation of the density-travel relationship \citep{ewing2018,ellder2022}. The closest antecedents of the rate, the mobility energy productivity metric \citep{hou2019} and carbon-based accessibility \citep{kinigadner2020}, are planning scores built on modelled travel times, energy intensities and emission factors; the rate here is an empirical national contrast on measured energy, read between dwelling forms and evaluated under decarbonisation scenarios. The neighbourhood-scale finding does not settle the metropolitan general-equilibrium question, in which relocation responses can offset densification gains \citep{gaigne2012}.

The scenario results identify the mechanism: uniform technology scales energy down without changing the structural quantities. Insulation lowers heat loss per square metre but leaves floor area unchanged, and electrification lowers energy per mile but leaves mileage unchanged. Full deployment of all three measures leaves \nepifullSurvives\% of the log gap; without heat pumps, insulation plus electrification leaves \nepisurvivesShare\%. Heat pumps, which reduce delivered home energy the most, leave the form gap marginally wider because the remainder is dominated by travel. The access deficit is unchanged in every scenario. This is lock-in in the strict sense \citep{unruh2000,seto2016}: the quantities that set the gap are fixed in built form and street layout for generations. For the whole technology set, the conclusion parallels what mobility-transition modelling finds for electrification alone: without demand reduction, vehicle-technology substitution is too slow for cumulative carbon budgets \citep{winkler2023}.

Whether a detached neighbourhood is more energy-intensive at all depends on the functional unit: per dwelling it consumes more, per person the gap narrows because detached homes hold larger households, and per square metre it narrows further and slightly reverses (\nepimSqGap$\times$). The specification adopted here lets the data determine the scaling instead of a fixed denominator (Methods). The rate is the quantity least exposed to the choice. Its access term is a property of the location, for which no per-person variant exists, and its energy term is the travel gap, which moves only from \nepitravelGap$\times$ to \nepitravelFamGap$\times$ at equal household size, so the access-per-kilowatt-hour conclusion does not depend on the functional unit.

For policy, the results identify two asymmetries in the current instrument set. First, the instruments address the smaller of the two energy gaps. The asset-level instruments (certificates, fabric subsidy and boiler replacement) operate on home energy, where the form gap is \nepiheatGap$\times$ and falls to \nepiheatSizeGap$\times$ at equal household size and floor area. The travel gap, \nepitravelGap$\times$ and robust to every ownership-allocation assumption down to \nepialphaZeroGap$\times$, has no dedicated asset-level instrument, because it is generated by the neighbourhood's location rather than by an asset a household owns. Second, the largest available energy saving is not scored by any instrument in the current set. A compact location provides \nepirate$\times$ the access per kilowatt-hour of driving, a larger relative differential between forms than any single technology measure produces on the energy axis. No certificate, subsidy or planning metric in current use records this differential, so the option that improves both quantities is absent from that accounting. A consumption assessment also misreads the scenario results: it shows the national programme succeeding, since absolute energy falls in every neighbourhood, while the relative position of places and the access deficit underlying it remain essentially unchanged. The same restriction rewards low-density form for its low per-capita home energy while ignoring its travel and access penalties.

A two-axis evaluation addresses both asymmetries: retrofit and electrification cannot supply proximity, and the rate expresses proximity in the same unit as those measures. Decarbonising buildings and vehicles alone does not close the gap; access must be planned and scored alongside energy. We refer to the two quantities and the rate between them as the neighbourhood energy performance index (NEPI). One caveat applies to the energy side: energy saved through efficiency is partly offset by rebound \citep{sorrell2007}, so dwelling-level gains need not scale to economy-wide reductions.

Revaluing access also carries a distributional implication. Access is capitalised into land prices and rents \citep{alonso1964}, but the present distribution in England runs against that expectation. The median area in the most income-deprived decile reaches about \nepiimdAccessRatio$\times$ the walkable amenities of the least deprived decile, which in turn uses \nepiimdEnergyRatio$\times$ the energy, because deprived households are concentrated in compact form and affluent households in dispersed form. Compact access is not at present a premium good at national scale. Within the strongest housing markets, however, the gradient flattens or inverts: across England access rises with income deprivation (rank correlation \nepiimdRhoNat), but in inner London the correlation falls to effectively zero ($\nepiimdRhoInner$) and it turns negative in Manchester ($\nepiimdRhoManchester$) and Bristol ($\nepiimdRhoBristol$), where the least deprived areas hold the highest-access cores and proximity already carries a price (Extended Data Fig.~4). The inversion is not explained by the overall affluence of the city: Manchester ranks among the most income-deprived cities in the comparison, yet its accessible core is held by its least-deprived neighbourhoods. The distributional risk is therefore visible wherever demand is strongest. Revaluing proximity without expanding its supply would price lower-income households out of the neighbourhoods the decarbonisation case favours, so planning for access requires expanding the supply of compact neighbourhoods as well as scoring the existing stock.

Four limitations concern the scope of the two axes. First, the energy axis counts car travel only, so public transport is absent from both axes in opposing directions: its energy is not charged to compact areas, whose residents travel further by bus and rail, and the stops that serve them are not credited as destinations in the access counts. The National Travel Survey bounds the energy omission, since it measures public-transport mileage by the same classes of residence as car mileage; only the energy per passenger-mile, which turns on vehicle load factors, has no measured counterpart. Charging that energy to residents leaves the total gap at \nepiptAssumedTotal$\times$ at an assumed \nepiptIntensityAssumed$\times$ the car intensity per passenger-mile, and at \nepiptCeilingTotal$\times$ (\nepiptCeilingTotalCI) even when every passenger-mile is priced as a mile driven alone in a petrol car (Extended Data Table~5). A fuller treatment of transport would draw on the national frequency-weighted public-transport accessibility indicators now available \citep{dftjts2021,verduzcotorres2024}. Second, the energy axis is confined to residential consumption, so it attributes to an area the metered domestic gas and electricity and the car travel of its residents rather than everything consumed within its boundary. Non-domestic load lies largely outside the metered series (Methods), so the commercial energy supporting the amenities that enter the access counts is charged to no neighbourhood, although it is concentrated in the compact areas that host those amenities and serves the residents of dispersed areas who drive to them. Third, these data measure compact form, not mixed use. Adding a jobs-housing balance measure, built from workplace jobs against resident households, to the same models leaves the rate unchanged at the scale where the measure is interpretable: a shift across the middle four-fifths of its distribution gives \nepimixBalanceRate$\times$ at Middle Layer Super Output Area scale, against \nepimixBalanceRateOa$\times$ at Output Area scale, where the measure is dominated by area size rather than by land use. The mixed-use element of the recommendation therefore rests on the planning literature rather than on this evidence; a land-use mix measure built from non-domestic floorspace by use class would be the test. Fourth, the analysis covers England only, pending a harmonised Welsh deprivation measure.

Five further limitations concern inference. First, the estimand is place-level, and the definitive self-selection test, which differences out fixed preferences with movers in panel microdata, is out of scope. Second, the analysis is ecological, at area rather than household level, with the usual inferential cautions \citep{robinson1950,greenland2001}. Third, the insulation measure assumes that the certificate potential-to-current ratio captures achievable fabric improvement. Fourth, the access counts treat amenity location as exogenous, though amenities partly follow demand; the near-convergence of counts at each area's own catchment is consistent with the compact advantage being one of proximity rather than of supply. Fifth, the access figures describe what residents can currently reach, and compact areas hold more residents per amenity, so the counts measure opportunity rather than a per-capita entitlement. The people gradient is reported so that the quotient can be formed: reachable jobs (\nepiwalkJobs$\times$) exceed reachable people (\nepiwalkPeople$\times$), so the jobs-per-resident contrast still favours compact form.

Assessed by access gained per unit of energy spent, compact form is the more efficient of the two. This answers the test the introduction drew from the Jacobs premise: between the extremes of England's morphological gradient, energy productivity is higher at the compact end. The difference persists until places are rebuilt, and no technology on the current deployment pathway reduces it. We therefore argue that decarbonisation strategy should place greater emphasis on compact urban form, and on the mixed-use development with which planning practice pairs it \citep{moreno2021}, though only the first of those is measured here. Compact form acts on the structural lock-in that the technology measures leave in place, and it improves energy and access together.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Unit of analysis}

The unit is the 2021 Census Output Area, the smallest published census geography, with about 125 households and 300 residents. Of England's 178,605 Output Areas, the \nepisampleN{} with usable metered energy (areas with more than ten residents and positive metered consumption) are analysed. Wales is excluded because the English Index of Multiple Deprivation, used as a control, has no directly comparable Welsh equivalent. All inputs are open data, processed to the Output Area on the British National Grid (EPSG:27700): Census 2021 topic tables (population, household size, dwelling-type shares, tenure, occupational class, car ownership, commute distance, workplace jobs); DESNZ sub-national gas and electricity statistics; domestic Energy Performance Certificates (floor area, build year, current and potential intensity, aggregated as area medians); OS Open Roads; amenity registers (Food Standards Agency, Get Information about Schools, NHS Organisation Data Service, OS Open Greenspace); the Indices of Deprivation 2025; DVLA vehicle licensing; National Travel Survey table NTS9904; and HadUK-Grid 1991--2020 heating-degree-days.

\subsection*{The energy axis}

Energy is delivered energy in kilowatt-hours per dwelling per year, not primary energy or carbon. The home component is metered: postcode-level gas and electricity, each supplied as a meter count and a mean per meter, are aggregated to the Output Area by meter-weighted means, summed, and divided by census households. Gas is weather-corrected. Both series are the domestic sector of the DESNZ statistics, but the two sectors are defined differently. Electricity is separated by meter profile class, the domestic sector comprising the non-half-hourly standard and Economy 7 profiles, with meters on those profiles reallocated to the commercial sector above 100,000 kWh per year. Gas carries no use-based flag: every meter consuming less than 73,200 kWh per year is classified as domestic, so small commercial premises remain in the domestic series. Such premises are concentrated in the compact areas that hold ground-floor commercial stock, and their consumption enters the numerator without adding to the household denominator, so the leakage acts against the reported form gradient. Metered energy is used because certificate ratings over-predict consumption, most severely for large detached dwellings, and would inflate the form gradient with model error \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}.

The travel component is the total car-travel energy of a location's residents, not the commute alone (about one sixth of car mileage). It is estimated by constrained disaggregation. NTS9904 gives measured car-driver miles per person by 2021 rural-urban class of residence, from about 2,500 miles per person per year (urban) to 5,200 (rural). Each class total is distributed across its Output Areas by an allocator combining cars per person (elasticity 1, the proportional headline) and commute distance (elasticity 0.30), constrained so that each class's population-weighted mean reproduces the survey figure exactly; only the within-class distribution is estimated, and both elasticities are swept in the sensitivity analysis. Miles are converted to energy at the local fleet intensity, \nepitravelIce{} kWh per mile (internal combustion) and \nepitravelEv{} (battery electric), blended by the DVLA battery-electric share. The intensities correspond to 0.58 and 0.20 kWh per vehicle-kilometre: fleet-average combustion consumption of about 6.5 litres per 100 km at 8.9 kWh per litre, and the real-world consumption of current battery-electric cars, consistent with the Department for Transport's energy-consumption and appraisal tables. Allocated per-person miles are multiplied by census household size to give miles per dwelling before conversion to energy, so household size is part of the per-dwelling travel contrast. Commute distance enters the allocator as the household-weighted mean of the census distance-band midpoints. Metered domestic electricity already includes home electric-vehicle charging, so the travel figure double-counts that overlap; at the current battery-electric fleet share (2--3\%) the effect is negligible. Public-transport mileage from the same survey table is disaggregated in the same way for the sensitivity reported in Extended Data Table~5, with the Census TS061 public-transport share of commuters as the within-class allocator; it does not enter the headline axis.

\subsection*{The access axis}

Access is the count of opportunities reachable from an Output Area over the road network. The England network (OS Open Roads, about 3.6 million junctions) is built once into a routable graph (cityseer \citep{simons2023}), and counts are accumulated outwards from each Output Area's nearest node at every distance band from 1,600\,m to 25,600\,m. Amenities are six everyday destination types: general practitioners and pharmacies (NHS), schools (GIAS), places to eat and drink and grocery shops (FSA), and public parks and greenspace (Ordnance Survey); sources, record counts and filters are given in Extended Data Table~1. Jobs are workplace jobs, each workplace weighted by its job count; people are the resident population. Each is read at 1,600\,m, at the area's own car catchment, and at 25,600\,m. Public-transport stops (NaPTAN bus stops and rail, metro and tram access points) are counted separately as a straight-line total within 1,600\,m and reported as context; a stop is a means of reach rather than a destination, so stops do not enter the amenity basket.

\subsection*{The rate}

For each Output Area, the rate is the amenity count within the area's own car catchment divided by its car-travel energy. The catchment is the area's allocated annual car-driver distance per person divided by 370 trips per person per year, approximating the National Travel Survey car-driver trip rate (table NTS0303: 363 in 2023, about 380 before the pandemic). The resulting one-way distance is used as the network reach, bounded to [1,600\,m, 25,600\,m]. Recomputing the catchments at 300 and 440 trips per year gives access advantages of \nepitripLowAdv$\times$ and \nepitripHighAdv$\times$ against the headline \nepitripMidAdv$\times$, and rates of \nepitripLowRate$\times$ and \nepitripHighRate$\times$ against \nepitripMidRate$\times$, so the level of the constant has little effect on the contrast. The flat-to-detached contrast of the rate is by construction the product of the access advantage (catchment amenities, flat:detached) and the energy saving (car-travel energy, detached:flat); it is reconstructable from Table~\ref{tab:axes}, not estimated as a separate model. The two terms are deliberately not independent: the catchment radius and the travel energy are both functions of how far residents drive, and this dependence is intended. The conditioning variants reported in the Results bound the rate's dependence on that construction.

\subsection*{The compositional model}

All contrasts derive from one compositional, no-intercept regression on each Output Area's dwelling-type shares (flat, terraced, semi-detached, detached, residual) as fractions summing to one. Without an intercept, each coefficient is the modelled outcome of a neighbourhood composed entirely of one type, and the ratio is $\exp(b_{\mathrm{detached}} - b_{\mathrm{flat}})$; the confounds enter uncentred and shift both pure-type predictions equally, so the ratio does not depend on the levels at which they are held. Regressions are household-weighted. The estimate is read at pure-composition vertices, which few areas approach, so each ratio states the contrast between idealised extremes. Support-respecting median counterparts are reported alongside: the ratio of medians between areas whose plurality dwelling type is flat and those whose plurality type is detached.

Energy enters in logarithms per dwelling, so fitted ratios are ratios of conditional geometric means. Household size and floor area are covariates with freely estimated coefficients, not denominators. Four confounds are held throughout: building age, deprivation (the overall Index of Multiple Deprivation and its income domain), tenure (social- and private-rented shares), and climate (heating-degree-days). Access counts are non-negative with frequent zeros and are fitted with a Poisson log link; with households as analytic weights the estimator is quasi-Poisson, using point estimates with a robust sandwich covariance. The access regression holds the income domain; it excludes density, which is the mechanism under study, and the overall deprivation index, whose barriers and living-environment sub-domains are themselves access measures.

Standard errors are clustered on the \nepiclusters{} local-authority districts, with $t(G-1)$ critical values; every reported ratio carries a 95\% interval on this basis. Composite quantities (the rate, the insulation-plus-electrification surviving-gap share, the mediated fraction) carry cluster-bootstrap intervals with 999 replications, resampling whole districts; the per-scenario ratios carry their own clustered intervals (Extended Data Table~2), from which the scenario closure shares derive.

\subsection*{Decarbonisation scenarios}

The energy axis is recomputed under the technology measures and the gap re-estimated. Insulation scales each home's metered gas by its certificate fabric-improvement ratio (modelled potential over current intensity, median \nepifabricFactorMedian, clipped to the unit interval; both terms are modelled, so the performance gap cancels); electricity is unchanged. Heat pumps deliver the gas heat as electricity at the delivered-energy factor of boiler efficiency over a seasonal coefficient of performance ($0.90/2.8 = 0.32$); a sensitivity analysis varies the coefficient between 2.4 and 3.2. Electrification recomputes car energy at electric fleet intensity with mileage unchanged. Both heat measures act on metered gas only, so electrically heated homes, more common among flats, receive no retrofit measure; this bias is conservative for the surviving-gap shares. Partial uptake blends transformed and untransformed energy linearly; the Balanced Pathway scenario uses the Climate Change Committee's 2040 stock shares \citep{ccc2025}. Every scenario is fitted on one common complete-case sample (\nepiscenarioN{} areas with a valid fabric ratio). Access is unchanged in every scenario, since no measure moves a destination.

\subsection*{Robustness and the estimand}

The estimand is place-level: the energy and access profile of a neighbourhood type conditional on observed confounds, not a household treatment effect. Three checks bound selection on unobservables. Access depends on location alone and is therefore unaffected by sorting. Occupational class (higher managerial and professional share) is added to the confounds and leaves the gaps essentially unchanged. A coefficient-stability bound \citep{oster2019} is computed on a continuous detached-share gradient in an intercept specification (the compositional model has no centred $R^2$), using the coefficient-based approximation with $R_{\max} = \min(1.3\tilde{R}, 1)$; the Results report $\delta^{*}$ together with the $\delta = 1$ bias-adjusted gap. The zonation check re-aggregates all variables, household-weighted, to LSOA and MSOA geographies and re-fits the identical model.
