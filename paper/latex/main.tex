\title[The energy productivity of neighbourhood form]{The energy productivity of neighbourhood form: everyday access per unit of energy across England}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Gareth} \sur{Simons}}\email{gareth@benchmarkurbanism.com}

\abstract{Neighbourhoods are conventionally assessed by the energy they consume, without reference to what that energy obtains. This paper measures the two quantities for \nepisampleN{} English Census Output Areas: everyday access (amenities, jobs and people reachable over the road network) and household energy (metered gas and electricity plus National Travel Survey-anchored car travel), together with the ratio between them, a measure of energy productivity for neighbourhoods. On foot, a flat-type neighbourhood reaches about \nepiwalkAmenRound{} times the amenities of a detached-type one when idealised pure types are compared; typical mixed areas show smaller gaps of the same order. At each area's own car catchment the reachable counts nearly converge, but the detached area closes the gap only through about three times the car-travel energy. Per kilowatt-hour of driving, a flat-type neighbourhood therefore obtains about \nepirate{} times the access. Total household energy per dwelling in detached areas is about \nepitotalGapRound{} times that of a flat-type area, largely reflecting the additional travel that dispersed form requires. The technology measures of the national decarbonisation strategy (insulation, heat pumps and electric vehicles) reduce consumption in every neighbourhood when fully deployed, yet close less than a third of the energy gap between forms and none of the access gap. The access deficit of dispersed form is structural and unaffected by the technology pathway; it therefore requires direct measurement and planning.}

\keywords{urban form, accessibility, energy, decarbonisation, compact city, neighbourhood}

Energy policy and neighbourhood assessment evaluate places by the energy they consume. Consumption alone, however, records nothing of what the energy obtains: the work, shops, schools and services within a household's reach. Economies are routinely assessed by their energy productivity, output per unit of energy input \citep{patterson1996}; no comparable measure exists for neighbourhoods, whose output is the daily activity their residents can reach.

This paper assesses neighbourhoods by access gained per unit of energy spent. The premise is ecological: a system extracts more function from each unit of energy when it is compact and complex, cycling energy through many close exchanges rather than allowing it to dissipate \citep{jacobs1969,jacobs2000}. Compact urban form supports such cycling, whereas dispersed form does not. The object of study is therefore the ratio between two measured quantities, energy spent and access gained (hereafter the rate). Neither quantity is informative alone: an energy measure classifies a remote dwelling with little within reach as efficient, and an access measure cannot distinguish proximity achieved through built density from the same reach obtained by additional driving. Distinguishing these cases requires the ratio.

Prior work treats the two quantities separately, and each strand carries a known measurement hazard. On the building side, compact forms lose less heat: simulation places the heat-demand contrast between detached and compact archetypes at up to sixfold \citep{rode2014}, while English national consumption statistics and metered analyses place detached gas consumption at about twice that of a flat \citep{need2024,ehs2024,wyatt2013}. The gap between those figures is largely the energy performance gap: certificate ratings are modelled and noisily assessed \citep{crawley2019}, over-predict consumption, and over-predict most for the largest dwellings \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}, so modelled energy inflates any form gradient. On the travel side, density is associated with lower car use \citep{newman1989}, but the marginal effect is modest \citep{ewing2017,echenique2012}, and the relationship is better read as one of accessibility than of density as such \citep{ewing2018,ellder2022}; at the metropolitan scale the compact-city case remains contested \citep{gaigne2012,mindali2004}. The functional-unit problem cuts across both strands: a low-density energy premium that is large per capita narrows or reverses per square metre \citep{norman2006}, because energy is sub-linear in household size \citep{druckman2008,huebner2017}; conclusions are therefore sensitive to the choice of denominator.

This separation is reproduced in policy, where the two families of instruments bearing on neighbourhood energy each address one of the quantities. The instruments of buildings and transport decarbonisation act on, and are evaluated by, the consumption of individual assets: fabric standards and retrofit subsidy keyed to the energy certificate, heat-pump deployment targets, and vehicle electrification mandates \citep{ukgovernment2023,ccc2025}. None of these instruments is informed by the asset's location: an identical fabric measure yields an identical rating improvement for a dwelling remote from every daily destination and for one adjacent to them. The internal weaknesses of this asset-level accounting are documented: certificate over-prediction \citep{summerfield2019,few2023} and the prebound gap between rated and actual consumption \citep{sunikka-blank2012}. The limitation examined here is external to the accounting. The travel demand generated by a dwelling's location is not represented in the assessment at all. Yet compact development measurably reduces driving \citep{trb2009,ewing2017,ewing2018}, and modelling of mobility transitions finds vehicle-technology substitution alone too slow to meet cumulative carbon budgets without demand reduction \citep{winkler2023}.

Planning approaches centred on accessibility address the second quantity without expressing it in energy terms. This line of work, from the critique of automobile dependence \citep{newman1999} through accessibility-based transport instruments \citep{rode2017} to the fifteen-minute city \citep{moreno2021}, targets proximity directly. A large mobility-data study finds that local amenity supply is the primary driver of local trip-making \citep{abbiasov2024}. Its metrics, however, are counts and travel times. Because the energy consequence of proximity is not quantified, proximity cannot enter the carbon accounting that allocates decarbonisation effort and cannot be compared with retrofit or electrification on a common unit. Its case is therefore made in terms of quality of life rather than of energy. The two literatures consequently share no common unit of evaluation; the ratio examined here supplies one.

Whether the consumption-centred strategy suffices is, moreover, an empirical question with a testable form. If the energy cost of dispersed form is structural, fixed in floor area and travel distance, then technology lowers consumption everywhere without closing the gap between forms, and the access deficit is unaffected. England provides a suitable test because its carbon budgets are legislated and the advised pathway to them is quantified \citep{ccc2025}, so the technology scenarios evaluated below correspond to the composition of the national strategy rather than to hypothetical policy bundles.

This paper measures both axes for \nepisampleN{} of England's 178,605 Census 2021 Output Areas: network-based access to amenities, jobs and people against metered home energy plus survey-anchored car-travel energy, with the rate between them. Energy is specified per dwelling, with household size and floor area as freely estimated controls, which resolves the functional-unit ambiguity. The closest antecedent to the rate is the mobility energy productivity metric \citep{hou2019}, a composite planning score weighting opportunity accessibility by modelled mode energy intensities for United States locations; a measured access-per-kilowatt-hour contrast between dwelling forms, built from metered energy at national scale, has, to our knowledge, no precedent. Because households select their dwellings \citep{cao2009,stevens2017}, the estimand is place-level, the profile of a neighbourhood type conditional on observed confounds, and its exposure to residential self-selection is bounded directly. Figure~\ref{fig:inversion} summarises the central result.

\section*{Results}

Every flat-to-detached contrast below derives from one compositional, no-intercept regression on each Output Area's dwelling-type mix, household-weighted, holding building age, deprivation, tenure and local climate constant (Methods). The contrast is read between idealised pure types, so it states the upper end of each gap; the gap between typical mixed areas is smaller, and both figures are reported. Confidence intervals are 95\%, clustered on \nepiclusters{} local-authority districts.

\subsection*{The access axis}

Access is the count of opportunities reachable from an area over the road network: amenities (six everyday destination types), jobs (each workplace weighted by its job count) and people (resident population). Each count is read at a short walk (1,600\,m, about twenty minutes on foot), at the area's own car catchment and at a long drive (25,600\,m) (Methods). Access is a property of the location rather than of its residents, so it does not depend on how the household is counted, and it is, by construction, unaffected by residential self-selection.

The access gap is largest at walking distance and remains open at every distance examined (Table~\ref{tab:axes}, Fig.~\ref{fig:reach}). On foot, a flat-type neighbourhood reaches \nepiwalkAmen$\times$ (95\% CI \nepiwalkAmenCI) the amenities, \nepiwalkJobs$\times$ (\nepiwalkJobsCI) the jobs and \nepiwalkPeople$\times$ (\nepiwalkPeopleCI) the people of a detached-type one. These are pure-type contrasts; between typical areas the on-foot amenity gap is \nepiwalkAmenDomMed$\times$ comparing dominant-type medians and \nepiwalkAmenSupport$\times$ comparing areas that are at least half of one type. Per-service counts at walking distance are given in Extended Data Fig.~3. At each area's own car catchment the raw counts nearly converge (amenities \nepicatchAmen$\times$, \nepicatchAmenCI), because a detached area's residents drive further, into a larger catchment. At a 25.6\,km drive, where a detached area's residents can reach denser places, the flat-type area still reaches \nepidriveAmen$\times$ the amenities, \nepidriveJobs$\times$ the jobs and \nepidrivePeople$\times$ the people. For context, a flat-type neighbourhood holds \nepipopDensityFlat{} residents per hectare, compared with \nepipopDensityDet{} in a detached-type one, a factor of \nepipopDensityRatio{} (dominant-type medians).

Figure~\ref{fig:map} maps the two quantities. By energy, dispersed high-consumption form covers most of England's land area and the compact cities form a low-consumption minority. By walkable access, the pattern reverses: high access is confined almost entirely to those same cities. The corresponding contrast within a single city, between a compact core and a dispersed edge, is shown in Extended Data Fig.~4, and the joint distribution over all individual areas in Extended Data Fig.~1.

\subsection*{The energy axis}

Household energy is delivered energy per dwelling per year, in two measured components: the home's metered gas and electricity, and the car-travel energy of its residents. The home component is metered rather than modelled, which avoids the performance gap; the travel component is estimated by disaggregating measured National Travel Survey mileage across areas under exact class constraints (Methods).

Total energy per dwelling in a detached neighbourhood is \nepitotalGap$\times$ (95\% CI \nepitotalGapCI) that of a flat-type one (Table~\ref{tab:axes}, Fig.~\ref{fig:energy}). The gradient is steepest in travel, \nepitravelGap$\times$ (\nepitravelGapCI); the metered home component is \nepiheatGap$\times$ (\nepiheatGapCI). The home-energy gap combines three consequences of low density: larger dwellings, larger households, and a more exposed envelope. Holding household size equal moves it to \nepiheatFamGap$\times$ (\nepiheatFamGapCI); holding floor area equal as well leaves \nepiheatSizeGap$\times$ (\nepiheatSizeGapCI), the direct effect of exposed walls and unshared surfaces. On the log scale, size and occupancy account for \nepimedShare\% (\nepimedShareCI\%) of the home-energy gap; this is a descriptive covariate adjustment rather than an identified mediation (Extended Data Fig.~2). The bivariate elasticity of home energy with respect to floor area is \nepifloorElast, and the household-size elasticity estimated within the model is \nepihhElast. Both are well below one, so no fixed denominator is used: dividing by residents or by floor area would impose an elasticity of one that the data reject.

Flat-type areas have fewer domestic gas meters than households (\nepimeterFlat{} per household against \nepimeterDet{} for detached), largely because all-electric flats heat with electricity, which the total captures. Communal heating metered as non-domestic is a genuine but smaller undercount. Restricting the sample to well-measured areas (gas-meter coverage at least 0.9) leaves the home-energy gap at \nepigasCovHeat$\times$, so the measurement issue, if anything, slightly understates the gap. The same restriction lowers the total gap to \nepigasCovTotal$\times$, a composition effect of the subsample, which excludes the off-gas rural detached areas with the highest travel energy.

\subsection*{The rate: access per kilowatt-hour}

The rate is the amenity count within the area's own car catchment divided by the area's car-travel energy; car energy is the denominator because it is the energy through which reach beyond walking distance is obtained. The flat-to-detached contrast of the rate is, by construction, the product of the two quantities already reported. At their own catchments the two forms reach a similar count (access advantage \nepicatchAmen$\times$), but the detached area does so with about three times the car energy (energy saving \nepitravelGap$\times$). Per kilowatt-hour, the flat-type area therefore obtains $\nepicatchAmen \times \nepitravelGap \approx \nepirate\times$ (95\% CI \nepirateCI) the access (Fig.~\ref{fig:rate}).

The value depends on how the two components are conditioned, and this dependence is reported. Matching the access side's income-only conditioning on the energy side raises the rate to \nepirateHarm$\times$ (\nepirateHarmCI). Computing the rate against already-electrified car-travel energy, which removes the dependence between driving distance and catchment size, lowers it to \nepirateCirc$\times$ (\nepirateCircCI); the dominant-type median rate is \nepirateDomMedian$\times$. The headline \nepirate$\times$ lies within this range, and the ordering is preserved under every variant considered.

The convergence of catchment counts is the reason that the two quantities require joint measurement. A count taken at each area's own car catchment shows the access gap as nearly closed; the accompanying energy is required to identify the additional driving through which the closure is achieved.

\subsection*{Decarbonisation scenarios and lock-in}

The scenario ladder is built from the principal technology measures of the national decarbonisation strategy, with the Balanced Pathway row corresponding to the pathway advised for the legislated carbon budgets \citep{ccc2025}. The ladder therefore evaluates the composition of the national strategy rather than a hypothetical programme. The energy axis is recomputed under each measure alone and in combination (Methods). Every scenario reduces absolute consumption in every neighbourhood, but none closes as much as a third of the energy gap between forms and none changes the access gap. The three measures act differently. Insulation scales each home's gas by its own certificate-derived fabric headroom, which is larger for detached homes, and closes more of the gap than any other single measure: fully deployed, it moves the total gap from \nepiscenarioBase$\times$ to \nepifabricGap$\times$, closing \nepifabricClosed\% of the log gap. Electrification recomputes car mileage at electric energy intensity and closes \nepievClosed\% of the log gap, leaving \nepievGap$\times$. Heat pumps reduce delivered home energy sharply but near-uniformly, which raises the relative weight of the larger travel gap; fully deployed they leave the total gap marginally wider, at \nepihpGap$\times$. On the Climate Change Committee's Balanced Pathway for 2040, with half of homes on heat pumps and three-quarters of cars electric \citep{ccc2025}, the gap is \nepicccGap$\times$, with \nepicccClosed\% closed. Full deployment of all three measures leaves the total gap at \nepifullGap$\times$, with \nepifullSurvives\% of the log gap surviving; insulation plus full electrification without heat pumps leaves \nepilockinGap$\times$, and \nepisurvivesShare\% (\nepisurvivesShareCI\%) survives. Confidence intervals for every scenario are given in Extended Data Table~2.

The access gap is unchanged in every scenario, because none of these technologies alters the distance between dwellings and destinations (Fig.~\ref{fig:scenarios}); the on-foot amenity contrast remains \nepiwalkAmenRound$\times$ throughout. The access deficit of dispersed form is fixed in the street layout and the location of destinations, which change over generations rather than over technology-replacement cycles.

\subsection*{Robustness}

Figure~\ref{fig:forest} presents every headline ratio with its clustered confidence interval. Three families of checks apply.

The first concerns residential self-selection. Households who choose detached homes may differ in unmeasured ways that also raise energy use, so the energy gaps are read as place-level profiles rather than household treatment effects. Adding occupational class to the confounds leaves the gaps essentially unchanged. A coefficient-stability bound \citep{oster2019} on a continuous detached-share specification gives $\delta^{*} \approx \nepiosterDeltaTotal$ for total energy, indicating that unobserved sorting would need to be slightly stronger than all measured confounds combined to nullify the gap. The bound indicates survival at the margin rather than insensitivity: at $\delta = 1$ the adjusted gap is \nepiosterBetaAdjTotal$\times$, and the home-energy component is weaker still ($\delta^{*} \approx \nepiosterDeltaHeat$; Extended Data Table~3). For this reason the argument rests on access and the rate, which are properties of location and unaffected by sorting by construction; the energy gap enters through the rate's denominator rather than as an independent causal claim.

The second concerns the travel construction. Because each rural-urban class total is anchored exactly to the measured survey figure, the two allocator assumptions only redistribute miles within a class. The commute-distance elasticity has little effect: varying it from 0.15 to 0.45 varies the travel contrast between \nepicommuteSweepLo$\times$ and \nepicommuteSweepHi$\times$ on dominant-type medians. The ownership elasticity is the more consequential assumption and is swept to its limiting value: the compositional travel gap is \nepitravelGap$\times$ under proportional allocation, \nepialphaEightGap$\times$ at an elasticity of 0.8, \nepialphaSixGap$\times$ at 0.6, and \nepialphaZeroGap$\times$ (\nepialphaZeroGapCI) at zero. At zero the ownership signal is removed; the between-class mileage gradient, the commute-distance term and household size remain. Under any ownership allocation the travel contrast remains at or above about two and a half times (95\% CI at the zero-elasticity limit \nepialphaZeroGapCI). At equal household size the travel gap is \nepitravelFamGap$\times$ (\nepitravelFamGapCI), so, unlike the home-energy gap, it is not primarily a household-size effect.

The third concerns measurement and zonation. The home-energy gap holds under meter-to-household denominator restrictions (\nepielecHeat--\nepigasCovHeat$\times$) and winsorisation (\nepiwinsorHeat$\times$). Re-aggregating all variables to the two coarser census geographies and re-fitting the same model leaves the total gap at \nepimaupTotalOa$\times$, \nepimaupTotalLsoa$\times$ and \nepimaupTotalMsoa$\times$ at Output Area, LSOA and MSOA scale, with the support-respecting median counterpart at \nepimaupMedOa$\times$, \nepimaupMedLsoa$\times$ and \nepimaupMedMsoa$\times$; the result is therefore not an artefact of the zonation. The home-energy vertex contrast attenuates towards parity at MSOA scale, where no unit approaches a pure composition, while the total gap, on which the argument rests, holds at every scale (Extended Data Table~4).

\section*{Discussion}

The component results agree with the published estimates where they overlap; the contribution therefore lies in the combination rather than in any single component. The metered home-energy gap of \nepiheatGap$\times$ agrees with national consumption statistics \citep{need2024,ehs2024} and dwelling-level metered regressions \citep{wyatt2013}, and lies well below simulation contrasts \citep{rode2014}, a difference attributable to the performance gap \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}. The dependence of the conclusion on the functional unit reproduces lifecycle results \citep{norman2006}, and the below-one household-size elasticity is consistent with the downsizing and household-composition literature \citep{huebner2017,druckman2008}. The travel contrast agrees in direction with the urban-form literature but exceeds marginal-effect estimates \citep{echenique2012} because it is a different estimand: a cross-sectional contrast between the extremes of the form distribution, read against the observed rural-urban mileage gradient. The access measurement operationalises the accessibility interpretation of the density-travel relationship \citep{ewing2018,ellder2022}. The closest antecedent of the rate, the mobility energy productivity metric \citep{hou2019}, is a prospective planning score built on modelled travel times and mode energy intensities; the rate here is an empirical national contrast on measured energy, read between dwelling forms and evaluated under decarbonisation scenarios. The neighbourhood-scale finding does not settle the metropolitan general-equilibrium question, in which relocation responses can offset densification gains \citep{gaigne2012}.

The scenario results identify the mechanism. Uniform technology scales energy down without changing the structural quantities: insulation lowers heat loss per square metre but leaves floor area unchanged, and electrification lowers energy per mile but leaves mileage unchanged. Full deployment of all three measures leaves \nepifullSurvives\% of the log gap; without heat pumps, insulation plus electrification leaves \nepisurvivesShare\%. Heat pumps, which reduce delivered home energy the most, leave the form gap marginally wider because the remainder is dominated by travel. The access deficit is unchanged in every scenario. This is lock-in in the strict sense \citep{unruh2000,seto2016}: the quantities that set the gap are fixed in built form and street layout for generations. The scenario results also indicate what each form of assessment records. A consumption assessment shows the national programme succeeding, since absolute energy falls in every neighbourhood. The rate shows that the same programme leaves the relative position of places, and the access deficit underlying it, essentially unchanged. An assessment framework restricted to consumption therefore provides no indication of the remaining problem.

Whether a detached neighbourhood is more energy-intensive at all depends on the functional unit, and the unit is a modelling choice rather than a presentational one. Per dwelling it consumes more; per person the gap narrows, because detached homes hold larger households; per square metre the gap narrows further and slightly reverses (0.92$\times$). Since the household-size elasticity of home energy is about \nepihhElast, per-person normalisation overstates the scaling and credits detached homes for housing the larger households that select into them. The specification adopted here, energy per dwelling with household size and floor area as freely estimated controls, allows the data to determine the scaling and keeps the selection observable.

For policy, the results identify two asymmetries in the current instrument set. First, the instruments address the smaller of the two energy gaps. The asset-level instruments (certificates, fabric subsidy and boiler replacement) operate on home energy, where the form gap is \nepiheatGap$\times$ and falls to \nepiheatSizeGap$\times$ at equal household size and floor area. The travel gap, \nepitravelGap$\times$ and robust to every ownership-allocation assumption down to \nepialphaZeroGap$\times$, has no asset-level instrument, because it is generated by the neighbourhood's location rather than by any asset a household owns. Second, the largest available energy saving is not scored by any existing instrument. A compact location provides \nepirate$\times$ the access per kilowatt-hour of driving, a larger relative differential between forms than any single technology measure produces on the energy axis. No certificate, subsidy or planning metric records this differential, so the option that improves both quantities is absent from the accounting that allocates decarbonisation effort. A neighbourhood rating restricted to consumption compounds the problem by rewarding low-density form for its low per-capita home energy while ignoring its travel and access penalties.

A two-axis measure addresses both asymmetries: it expresses proximity, which retrofit and electrification cannot supply, in the same unit as the measures with which proximity competes for resources. Decarbonising buildings and vehicles does not close the gap on its own; access must be planned and scored alongside energy. We refer to this measure, the two quantities and the rate between them, as the neighbourhood energy performance index (NEPI). It scores a neighbourhood in the way an energy performance certificate scores a building, with one addition. Alongside the current score, a technology-potential score can be computed under full deployment. The distance between the two, substantial for energy and nil for access, expresses the lock-in for each neighbourhood individually. Two caveats apply. Energy saved through efficiency is partly offset by rebound \citep{sorrell2007}, so dwelling-level gains need not scale to economy-wide reductions. Access is also capitalised into land prices and rents \citep{alonso1964}, so the advantages of compact form are not shared evenly; an access score should be read alongside distributional evidence rather than as a welfare conclusion.

Five limitations qualify the result. The estimand is place-level, and the definitive self-selection test, differencing out fixed preferences with movers in panel microdata, is out of scope. The analysis is ecological, at area rather than household level, with the usual inferential cautions \citep{robinson1950,greenland2001}. The insulation measure assumes that the certificate potential-to-current ratio captures achievable fabric improvement. The access counts treat amenity location as exogenous, though amenities partly follow demand; the near-convergence of counts at each area's own catchment is consistent with the compact advantage being one of proximity rather than of supply. The access figures describe what residents can currently reach. Relatedly, compact areas also hold more residents per amenity: the counts measure opportunity rather than a per-capita entitlement, and the people gradient is reported so that the quotient can be formed. Reachable jobs (\nepiwalkJobs$\times$) exceed reachable people (\nepiwalkPeople$\times$), so the jobs-per-resident contrast still favours compact form. Finally, the analysis covers England only, pending a harmonised Welsh deprivation measure.

Assessed by access gained per unit of energy spent, compact form is the more efficient of the two. The difference persists until places are rebuilt, and no technology on the current deployment pathway reduces it.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Unit of analysis}

The unit is the 2021 Census Output Area, the smallest published census geography, with about 125 households and 300 residents. Of England's 178,605 Output Areas, the \nepisampleN{} with usable metered energy (areas with more than ten residents and positive metered consumption) are analysed. Wales is excluded because the English Index of Multiple Deprivation, used as a control, has no directly comparable Welsh equivalent. All inputs are open data, processed to the Output Area on the British National Grid (EPSG:27700): Census 2021 topic tables (population, household size, dwelling-type shares, tenure, occupational class, car ownership, commute distance, workplace jobs); DESNZ sub-national gas and electricity statistics; domestic Energy Performance Certificates (floor area, build year, current and potential intensity, aggregated as area medians); OS Open Roads; amenity registers (Food Standards Agency, Get Information about Schools, NHS Organisation Data Service, OS Open Greenspace); the Indices of Deprivation 2025; DVLA vehicle licensing; National Travel Survey table NTS9904; and HadUK-Grid 1991--2020 heating-degree-days.

\subsection*{The energy axis}

Energy is delivered energy in kilowatt-hours per dwelling per year, not primary energy or carbon. The home component is metered: postcode-level gas and electricity, each supplied as a meter count and a mean per meter, are aggregated to the Output Area by meter-weighted means, summed, and divided by census households. Gas is weather-corrected; both series cover domestic meters only. Metered energy is used because certificate ratings over-predict consumption, most severely for large detached dwellings, and would inflate the form gradient with model error \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}.

The travel component is the total car-travel energy of a location's residents, not the commute alone (about one sixth of car mileage). It is estimated by constrained disaggregation. NTS9904 gives measured car-driver miles per person by 2021 rural-urban class of residence, from about 2,500 miles per person per year (urban) to 5,200 (rural). Each class total is distributed across its Output Areas by an allocator combining cars per person (elasticity 1, the proportional headline) and commute distance (elasticity 0.30), constrained so that each class's population-weighted mean reproduces the survey figure exactly; only the within-class distribution is estimated, and both elasticities are swept in the sensitivity analysis. Miles are converted to energy at the local fleet intensity, \nepitravelIce{} kWh per mile (internal combustion) and \nepitravelEv{} (battery electric), blended by the DVLA battery-electric share. The intensities correspond to 0.58 and 0.20 kWh per vehicle-kilometre: fleet-average combustion consumption of about 6.5 litres per 100 km at 8.9 kWh per litre, and the real-world consumption of current battery-electric cars, consistent with the Department for Transport's energy-consumption and appraisal tables. Allocated per-person miles are multiplied by census household size to give miles per dwelling before conversion to energy, so household size is part of the per-dwelling travel contrast. Commute distance enters the allocator as the household-weighted mean of the census distance-band midpoints. Metered domestic electricity already includes home electric-vehicle charging, so the travel figure double-counts that overlap; at the current battery-electric fleet share (2--3\%) the effect is negligible.

\subsection*{The access axis}

Access is the count of opportunities reachable from an Output Area over the road network. The England network (OS Open Roads, about 3.6 million junctions) is built once into a routable graph (cityseer \citep{simons2023}), and counts are accumulated outwards from each Output Area's nearest node at every distance band from 1,600\,m to 25,600\,m. Amenities are six everyday destination types: general practitioners and pharmacies (NHS), schools (GIAS), food outlets and food shops (FSA), and public parks and greenspace (Ordnance Survey); sources, record counts and filters are given in Extended Data Table~1. Jobs are workplace jobs, each workplace weighted by its job count; people are the resident population. Each is read at 1,600\,m, at the area's own car catchment, and at 25,600\,m.

\subsection*{The rate}

For each Output Area, the rate is the amenity count within the area's own car catchment divided by its car-travel energy. The catchment is the area's allocated annual car-driver distance per person divided by 370 trips per person per year, approximating the National Travel Survey car-driver trip rate (table NTS0303: 363 in 2023, about 380 before the pandemic). The resulting one-way distance is used as the network reach, bounded to [1,600\,m, 25,600\,m]. Recomputing the catchments at 300 and 440 trips per year gives access advantages of \nepitripLowAdv$\times$ and \nepitripHighAdv$\times$ against the headline \nepitripMidAdv$\times$, and rates of \nepitripLowRate$\times$ and \nepitripHighRate$\times$ against \nepitripMidRate$\times$, so the level of the constant has little effect on the contrast. The flat-to-detached contrast of the rate is by construction the product of the access advantage (catchment amenities, flat:detached) and the energy saving (car-travel energy, detached:flat); it is reconstructable from Table~\ref{tab:axes}, not estimated as a separate model. The two terms are deliberately not independent: the catchment radius and the travel energy are both functions of how far residents drive, and this dependence is intended. The conditioning variants reported in the Results bound the rate's dependence on that construction.

\subsection*{The compositional model}

All contrasts derive from one compositional, no-intercept regression on each Output Area's dwelling-type shares (flat, terraced, semi-detached, detached, residual) as fractions summing to one. Without an intercept, each coefficient is the modelled outcome of a neighbourhood composed entirely of one type, and the ratio is $\exp(b_{\mathrm{detached}} - b_{\mathrm{flat}})$; the confounds enter uncentred and shift both pure-type predictions equally, so the ratio does not depend on the levels at which they are held. Regressions are household-weighted. The estimate is read at pure-composition vertices, which few areas approach, so each ratio states the contrast between idealised extremes. Support-respecting median counterparts are reported alongside: the ratio of medians between areas whose plurality dwelling type is flat and those whose plurality type is detached.

Energy enters in logarithms per dwelling, so fitted ratios are ratios of conditional geometric means. Household size and floor area are covariates with freely estimated coefficients, not denominators. Four confounds are held throughout: building age, deprivation (the overall Index of Multiple Deprivation and its income domain), tenure (social- and private-rented shares), and climate (heating-degree-days). Access counts are non-negative with frequent zeros and are fitted with a Poisson log link; with households as analytic weights the estimator is quasi-Poisson, using point estimates with a robust sandwich covariance. The access regression holds the income domain; it excludes density, which is the mechanism under study, and the overall deprivation index, whose barriers and living-environment sub-domains are themselves access measures.

Standard errors are clustered on the \nepiclusters{} local-authority districts, with $t(G-1)$ critical values; every reported ratio carries a 95\% interval on this basis. Composite quantities (the rate, the insulation-plus-electrification surviving-gap share, the mediated fraction) carry cluster-bootstrap intervals with 999 replications, resampling whole districts; the per-scenario ratios carry their own clustered intervals (Extended Data Table~2), from which the scenario closure shares derive.

\subsection*{Decarbonisation scenarios}

The energy axis is recomputed under the technology measures and the gap re-estimated. Insulation scales each home's metered gas by its certificate fabric-improvement ratio (modelled potential over current intensity, median \nepifabricFactorMedian, clipped to the unit interval; both terms are modelled, so the performance gap cancels); electricity is unchanged. Heat pumps deliver the gas heat as electricity at the delivered-energy factor of boiler efficiency over a seasonal coefficient of performance ($0.90/2.8 = 0.32$); a sensitivity analysis varies the coefficient between 2.4 and 3.2. Electrification recomputes car energy at electric fleet intensity with mileage unchanged. Both heat measures act on metered gas only, so electrically heated homes, more common among flats, receive no retrofit measure; this bias is conservative for the surviving-gap shares. Partial uptake blends transformed and untransformed energy linearly; the Balanced Pathway scenario uses the Climate Change Committee's 2040 stock shares \citep{ccc2025}. Every scenario is fitted on one common complete-case sample (\nepiscenarioN{} areas with a valid fabric ratio). Access is unchanged in every scenario, since no measure moves a destination.

\subsection*{Robustness and the estimand}

The estimand is place-level: the energy and access profile of a neighbourhood type conditional on observed confounds, not a household treatment effect. Three checks bound selection on unobservables. Access depends on location alone and is therefore unaffected by sorting. Occupational class (higher managerial and professional share) is added to the confounds and leaves the gaps essentially unchanged. A coefficient-stability bound \citep{oster2019} is computed on a continuous detached-share gradient in an intercept specification (the compositional model has no centred $R^2$), using the coefficient-based approximation with $R_{\max} = \min(1.3\tilde{R}, 1)$; the Results report $\delta^{*}$ together with the $\delta = 1$ bias-adjusted gap. The zonation check re-aggregates all variables, household-weighted, to LSOA and MSOA geographies and re-fits the identical model.
