\abstract{Decarbonisation policy reduces the energy that homes and vehicles consume through mitigation measures such as insulation, heat pumps, and electric vehicles. How much energy a neighbourhood needs, however, is also affected by its form: compact neighbourhoods lose less heat through shared walls, and their residents drive less because destinations lie nearer. Whether measures applied to individual homes and vehicles can cancel out the energy premium of dispersed form is therefore an open question. If they cannot, that premium will persist for the life of the building stock. We compare neighbourhoods across England's full range of dwelling mix, from wholly flats at the compact end to wholly detached houses at the dispersed end. The energy data come from metered gas and electricity for the home and National Travel Survey mileage for the car, across \nepisampleN{} Census 2021 Output Areas, holding building age, deprivation, tenure, and local climate constant. The most dispersed spend \nepiheatGap{} times as much energy on their homes as the most compact, and \nepitravelGap{} times as much on car travel, giving \nepitotalGapRound{} times as much in total per dwelling, or \nepifamNowGap{} times at equal household size. When the mitigation measures are applied in full, insulating every home to its certificate potential and electrifying every car, consumption falls in every neighbourhood, and the gap remains at \nepilockinGap{} times. Dispersed development adds an energy premium that remains locked in for the life of the building stock. Greater attention to compact urban form is therefore necessary in energy policy, above all for new development.}

\keywords{urban form, accessibility, energy, decarbonisation, compact city, neighbourhood}

Energy and decarbonisation policy evaluates progress by the energy that homes and vehicles consume and the carbon they emit, and it targets the same assets with insulation, heat pumps, and electric vehicles \citep{ukgovernment2023,ccc2025}. The energy those homes and vehicles need, however, also depends on the form of the city around them. Dwellings in a more compact neighbourhood share more of their building envelope, so they lose less heat. Destinations also lie nearer, which reduces car use and the energy spent on travel. The morphology behind that demand receives little attention in energy policy. This approach may well suit the existing stock, whose form is already fixed, therefore requiring emphasis on upgrades to the buildings and vehicles themselves; however, it risks leaving a blind spot in new urban development, where---even with insulation, heat pumps, and electric vehicles fully deployed---what is built now may exact an energy premium that remains locked in for the life of the building stock. Whether these upgrades can offset the premium of dispersed form is therefore an open question.

The conceptual premise is that compact urban form affords higher energy productivity than dispersed urban form. This is inspired by Jane Jacobs's account of ecosystems and economies as conduits for energy \citep{jacobs2000}: the more compact, complex, and diverse a system, the more capable it is of harnessing and recycling a given unit of energy before that unit is lost. It therefore obtains more use from the same amount of energy, in effect for free; we refer to this as the energy productivity principle. Jacobs illustrated the idea with a rainforest and a desert. The rainforest's complex web of interdependencies allows it to harness a unit of incoming energy and recycle it through successive cascades of species interdependencies, extracting maximal use before that unit of energy is lost from the system. The desert lacks that complexity, so it cannot recycle the unit and extracts far less use from an equivalent amount of energy. She applied the framing to economies \citep{jacobs1969,jacobs2000}, and her urban themes of density, functional diversity, and interconnectedness can be seen as an expression of a similar intuition \citep{jacobs1961}. Applied to cities, the principle implies that energy productivity \citep{patterson1996} should rise with compactness. The urban scaling literature quantifies a related regularity, in which incomes, innovation, and aspects of quality of life improve faster than population as cities grow, while infrastructure and services are delivered with less per person \citep{bettencourt2007,glaeser2011}. Scaling, however, concerns the size of cities, which tracks compactness only loosely, and its gains are counted per person or in monetary terms rather than in energy. Following the recycling of energy through every cascading dependency is an impossibly complex task. At the neighbourhood scale, however, it becomes possible to use a more localised form of analysis to disentangle the relationship between energy and compactness, by evaluating the energy a given area spends on homes and travel. In this case, Jacobs's energy productivity can be interpreted literally, with energy as its unit. In theory, for a given unit of energy, a more compact neighbourhood should house more households, and those households should gain greater access to amenities and services. In that comparison, the dispersed neighbourhood is the desert, extracting less use from an equivalent amount of energy.

The energy productivity of a neighbourhood is not a quantity the policy instruments in current use are built to register. They are aimed at individual assets: fabric standards and retrofit subsidy keyed to the energy certificate, heat-pump deployment targets, and vehicle electrification mandates \citep{ukgovernment2023,ccc2025}. These instruments are usually criticised for accuracy, since certificate ratings routinely over-predict the energy that homes use \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,sunikka-blank2012}. The criticism made here concerns what the instruments measure rather than their accuracy. On the building side, a certificate rates a dwelling against its own fabric and services, per square metre of floor area. A compact home and a dispersed one can therefore carry the same rating while consuming different amounts of energy, because the rating is normalised by floor area and the dispersed home is larger. On the travel side, dispersed urban form sets the distances residents drive, yet policy focuses mainly on vehicle efficiency rather than on those distances. Compact development reduces driving \citep{trb2009,ewing2018}, and local amenity supply is the primary determinant of trip-making \citep{abbiasov2024}. Cities where more destinations lie within a walk emit less transport carbon per person \citep{marzolla2025}. Modelling suggests that electrifying cars without reducing driving cuts emissions too slowly to stay within carbon budgets \citep{winkler2023}. Planning has long attended to neighbourhood form, from the critique of automobile dependence to compact cities and the fifteen-minute city \citep{newman1999,rode2017,moreno2021}, but its metrics are chiefly counts and travel times. Neither approach conventionally states what a neighbourhood's compactness is worth in energy.

To state that worth, we combine the planning literature's attention to compact urban form with the energy metrics of decarbonisation policy. We compare neighbourhoods from flats through terraced and semi-detached houses to detached ones, for the whole of England at neighbourhood resolution, so that energy and access can be followed across the full range of compactness. Rather than relying on models, we measure both quantities for each neighbourhood from records: energy as the metered consumption of its homes plus the car travel of its residents, and access as the count of destinations within their reach. Together they express the use each neighbourhood obtains from the energy it spends. The energy productivity principle predicts that this use should rise with compactness. We then apply the mitigation measures in full, insulating every home and electrifying every car, and read the premium that remains as the lock-in.

Each half of this design has a precedent in earlier work. The first comes from transport and accessibility research, which developed the idea of valuing access by what it costs to obtain. Hou and colleagues constructed the mobility energy productivity metric to measure the quality of mobility a United States location affords \citep{hou2019}. It counts the opportunities reachable from a place, weighted by the time, money, and energy required. Kinigadner and colleagues inverted the budget, asking how many destinations can be reached within a fixed emission allowance \citep{kinigadner2020}. In full cost accessibility, Cui and Levinson brought crash risk and emissions into the monetised travel cost against which accessibility is computed \citep{cui2018}. All three are appraisal tools for locations and proposals, and their energy terms are modelled rather than measured.

The second comes from energy research, which measures what households consume. Chatterton and colleagues mapped direct energy use across the 34,753 Lower-layer Super Output Areas of England and Wales, neighbourhoods averaging 1,600 residents, joining metered gas and electricity with car energy from odometer readings \citep{chatterton2016}. Their purpose was distributional: a minority of areas consume far more than the rest, and carbon policy, they argued, should attend to high consumers as well as the energy poor. They also arrived at this paper's question from the energy side, asking whether high consumption reflects need or choice, with housing type and poor accessibility among the candidates, and framing the constrained case as lock-in. Answering that question requires a measure of access, which we contribute here, and a finer unit of analysis, so that neighbourhoods can be distinguished by their compactness. For this purpose we adopt the Output Area, one census level below their Super Output Areas; at about 125 households, it is small enough for a single dwelling type to dominate.

The comparison between compact and dispersed forms is subject to three hazards. The first is how home energy is measured. Modelled energy exaggerates the contrast between forms: simulation places the heat-demand gap between detached and compact archetypes at up to sixfold \citep{rode2014}, while metered analyses put detached gas consumption at about twice that of a flat \citep{need2024,ehs2024,wyatt2013}. The exaggeration arises because certificate ratings are modelled and noisily assessed \citep{crawley2019}, and they over-predict consumption most for the largest dwellings \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}. Home energy therefore comes from meters. The second hazard is inferring travel from density. Density is associated with lower car use \citep{newman1989}, but the effect is modest \citep{ewing2017,echenique2012}, contested at the metropolitan scale \citep{gaigne2012,mindali2004}, and better read as one of accessibility \citep{ewing2018,ellder2022}. Travel energy therefore comes from measured national mileage, with the allocation assumptions swept in the robustness analysis, and access is counted over the road network rather than proxied by density.

The third hazard is the choice of denominator. Energy per person, per dwelling, and per square metre give different verdicts: a low-density premium that is large per capita narrows or reverses per square metre \citep{norman2006}. Two people sharing a home use less than twice the energy of one \citep{druckman2008,huebner2017}, and a large home spreads its heating over more floor area, so dividing by persons or by square metres rewards the larger household or the larger home. Energy therefore enters per dwelling, and household size and floor area enter as controls, with their effects estimated from the data rather than fixed by the choice of denominator. A final caution concerns who lives where. Households choose their dwellings \citep{cao2009,stevens2017}, so the comparison is between places rather than between households, describing a neighbourhood type conditional on the observed confounds. Its exposure to residential self-selection is bounded with a coefficient-stability test. Figure~\ref{fig:inversion} shows that comparison, setting energy against access for the four dwelling types.

\section*{Results}

At issue is whether measures applied to individual homes and vehicles can cancel the energy premium of dispersed urban form, or whether the premium persists for the life of the building stock. We answer through energy productivity, the use a neighbourhood obtains from the energy it spends, measured across the \nepisampleN{} of England's 178,605 Census 2021 Output Areas with usable metered energy. Real neighbourhoods lie on a spectrum of housing mixtures between two pure kinds, one wholly flats and one wholly detached houses. We fit each Output Area's dwelling mix in one compositional regression, holding building age, deprivation, tenure, and local climate constant, and recover the two pure ends of the spectrum (Methods). Confidence intervals are 95\%, clustered on \nepiclusters{} local-authority districts.

\subsection*{Energy}

The comparison begins with home energy, where the policy instruments concentrate. Home energy, measured at the meter rather than modelled, shows a gap between forms of \nepiheatGap$\times$ (95\% CI \nepiheatGapCI), well below the contrasts that simulation implies, because modelled ratings over-predict most for large dwellings (Methods). The gap has three parts: larger dwellings, larger households, and a more exposed envelope. Holding household size equal moves it to \nepiheatFamGap$\times$ (\nepiheatFamGapCI), and holding floor area equal as well leaves \nepiheatSizeGap$\times$ (\nepiheatSizeGapCI), which we read as the price of the unshared envelope.

The larger share of the difference lies in travel. Anchored to the measured mileage of each settlement class (Methods), the car-travel energy of a detached neighbourhood is \nepitravelGap$\times$ (\nepitravelGapCI) that of a flat-type one. Unlike the home component, the contrast is not primarily a household-size effect; at equal household size it remains \nepitravelFamGap$\times$. Together the components put total energy per dwelling at \nepitotalGap$\times$ (\nepitotalGapCI), or \nepifamNowGap$\times$ at equal household size (Table~\ref{tab:axes}). Read the other way, the energy that houses one detached-type household houses about two flat-type households.

\subsection*{Access}

The purpose of the driving is reach, and reach is where the two forms differ most. On foot, within 1,600\,m over the road network (a twenty-minute walk), a flat-type neighbourhood reaches \nepiwalkAmen$\times$ the amenities, \nepiwalkJobs$\times$ the jobs, and \nepiwalkPeople$\times$ the people of a detached-type one (95\% CIs in Fig.~\ref{fig:forest}); between typical mixed areas the amenity gap is \nepiwalkAmenDomMed$\times$ to \nepiwalkAmenSupport$\times$ (per-service counts in Extended Data Fig.~2). On the map, dispersed high-energy form covers most of England's land area and walkable access is confined to the compact cities (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}; Extended Data Figs.~1 and~3). Driving narrows the difference: at each area's own car catchment, the distance its residents actually drive, the counts nearly converge (amenities \nepicatchAmen$\times$, \nepicatchAmenCI). Residents of dispersed areas extend their driving until their access is equivalent, and the equivalence costs about three times the car-travel energy of a flat-type area (Table~\ref{tab:axes}). Even at a 25.6\,km drive, the flat-type area still reaches \nepidriveAmen$\times$ the amenities, \nepidriveJobs$\times$ the jobs, and \nepidrivePeople$\times$ the people (Table~\ref{tab:axes}, Fig.~\ref{fig:reach}).

\subsection*{The rate: access per kilowatt-hour}

The trade just seen, similar counts at three times the energy, is the reason access and energy must be read together. The rate joins the two quantities into an energy productivity for places. It divides the amenity count within the area's own car catchment by the area's car-travel energy; car energy is the denominator because it is the energy through which access beyond walking distance is obtained. Its factors are the two numbers just reported: the near-equal counts (access advantage \nepicatchAmen$\times$) and the threefold car energy (energy saving \nepitravelGap$\times$). Per kilowatt-hour, the flat-type area therefore obtains $\nepicatchAmen \times \nepitravelGap \approx \nepirate\times$ (95\% CI \nepirateCI) the access (Fig.~\ref{fig:rate}).

Conditioning variants move the rate between \nepirateCirc$\times$ (\nepirateCircCI) and \nepirateHarm$\times$ (\nepirateHarmCI), with the dominant-type median at \nepirateDomMedian$\times$, and the ordering of forms holds in every variant (Methods). The lower bound takes already-electrified car travel as its denominator, which removes the link between driving distance and catchment size.

\subsection*{Decarbonisation scenarios and lock-in}

The national strategy, carried through in full, narrows the energy gap only part way and leaves the access gap untouched. The scenario ladder recomputes energy under each of its measures, alone and in combination, while access stays as measured (Methods; Fig.~\ref{fig:scenarios}). Every scenario reduces absolute consumption in every neighbourhood, but the three measures act differently on the gap between forms. Fully deployed insulation closes more of the gap than any other single measure, moving the total gap from \nepiscenarioBase$\times$ to \nepifabricGap$\times$ (\nepifabricClosed\% of the log gap). Vehicle electrification closes \nepievClosed\%, leaving \nepievGap$\times$. Heat pumps cut home energy sharply but near-uniformly, which raises the relative weight of the larger travel gap and leaves the total marginally wider, at \nepihpGap$\times$. The Climate Change Committee's Balanced Pathway for 2040, advised for the legislated carbon budgets, sets half of homes on heat pumps and three-quarters of cars electric \citep{ccc2025}; at those uptakes the gap is \nepicccGap$\times$ (\nepicccClosed\% closed). Full deployment of all three leaves \nepifullGap$\times$, with \nepifullSurvives\% surviving (confidence intervals in Extended Data Table~2).

The headline lock-in bound is read at fully deployed insulation plus vehicle electrification, without heat pumps. Heat pumps switch the fuel of delivered heat rather than reducing the demand for it, and so widen the gap. Excluding them grants the technology pathway its most favourable case. Under that bound the total gap is \nepilockinGap$\times$ (\nepilockinGapCI), with \nepisurvivesShare\% (\nepisurvivesShareCI\%) of the log gap surviving; at equal household size the same measures move \nepifamNowGap$\times$ to \nepifamOptGap$\times$. The access deficit of dispersed form is untouched by any scenario, because it is fixed in the street layout and the location of destinations, which change over generations rather than over technology-replacement cycles.

\subsection*{Robustness}

The gaps could in principle be artefacts of residential sorting, of the mileage allocation, of the metering, or of the zoning unit, and their intervals could be too narrow if dependence crosses district boundaries. Each check moves the estimates little, and none reverses them. Access is a property of the location rather than of its residents, so residential sorting cannot create its gap. Households who choose detached homes may differ in unmeasured ways, so the energy gaps are read as place-level profiles rather than household effects. Adding occupational class changes them little, and a coefficient-stability bound \citep{oster2019} gives $\delta^{*} \approx \nepiosterDeltaTotal$ for total energy, survival at the margin rather than insensitivity; the home-energy component is weaker still (Extended Data Table~3). In the travel construction, class mileage is anchored to the measured survey totals, and sweeping the within-class allocator to its limit leaves the travel gap at or above \nepialphaZeroGap$\times$ (\nepialphaZeroGapCI); charging public transport to residents on the same construction leaves the total gap at \nepiptAssumedTotal$\times$ (\nepiptAssumedTotalCI) (Extended Data Tables~5 and~6). In measurement, flat-type areas hold fewer domestic gas meters than households, and the coverage restrictions that address it leave the home-energy gap slightly higher, so the bias understates the gap (Extended Data Table~6). In zonation, re-fitting the same model at the two coarser census geographies leaves the total gap at \nepimaupTotalLsoa$\times$ and \nepimaupTotalMsoa$\times$, so the result is not an artefact of the unit, although the home-energy contrast alone attenuates at the coarsest scale (Extended Data Table~4). In inference, we merged the districts into fifty contiguous spatial blocks to check that dependence crossing their boundaries is contained. The total-gap interval widens only to \nepispatialBlockGapCI, the estimate is unchanged, and clustering on districts is therefore sufficient. Figure~\ref{fig:forest} collects every headline ratio with its clustered confidence interval.

\section*{Discussion}

The component results agree with the published estimates where they overlap, so the contribution lies in the combination. The metered home-energy gap of \nepiheatGap$\times$ agrees with national consumption statistics and metered regressions \citep{need2024,ehs2024,wyatt2013} and lies well below simulation contrasts \citep{rode2014}, a difference attributable to the performance gap \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}. The travel contrast exceeds marginal-effect estimates \citep{echenique2012} because it is a different estimand, a cross-sectional contrast between the extremes of the form distribution read against the observed rural-urban mileage gradient. The closest antecedents of the rate \citep{hou2019,kinigadner2020} are planning scores built on modelled inputs; the rate here is a national contrast on measured energy, evaluated under decarbonisation scenarios.

The scenario results identify the mechanism: uniform technology scales energy down without changing the structural quantities. Insulation lowers heat loss per square metre but leaves floor area unchanged, and electrification lowers energy per mile but leaves mileage unchanged. This is lock-in in the strict sense \citep{unruh2000,seto2016}, fixed in built form and street layout for generations, and it parallels mobility-transition modelling of electrification alone, where vehicle-technology substitution without demand reduction is too slow for cumulative carbon budgets \citep{winkler2023}. Per square metre the home-energy gap narrows and slightly reverses (\nepimSqGap$\times$), so the functional unit is a genuine choice, and the specification lets the data determine the scaling (Methods). The rate is the quantity least exposed to that choice, since its access term has no per-person variant and its travel term moves only from \nepitravelGap$\times$ to \nepitravelFamGap$\times$ at equal household size.

For policy, the results identify two asymmetries in the current instrument set. The asset-level instruments (certificates, fabric subsidy, and boiler replacement) operate on home energy, the smaller of the two energy gaps (Table~\ref{tab:axes}); the travel gap has no dedicated instrument, because it is generated by the neighbourhood's location rather than by an asset a household owns. And the largest available differential is not scored at all: a compact location provides \nepirate$\times$ the access per kilowatt-hour of driving, yet no certificate, subsidy, or planning metric in current use records it, so the option that improves both quantities is absent from the accounting. Evaluating access alongside energy addresses both asymmetries, since retrofit and electrification cannot supply proximity and the rate expresses proximity in the same unit as those measures. Access must be planned and scored alongside energy, and we refer to the two quantities and the rate between them as the neighbourhood energy performance index (NEPI).

Revaluing access also carries a distributional implication. Access is capitalised into land prices and rents \citep{alonso1964}, but the present distribution in England runs against that expectation: the median area in the most income-deprived decile reaches about \nepiimdAccessRatio$\times$ the walkable amenities of the least deprived decile, which in turn uses \nepiimdEnergyRatio$\times$ the energy, because deprived households are concentrated in compact form and affluent households in dispersed form. In the strongest housing markets the gradient inverts. Across England access rises with income deprivation (rank correlation \nepiimdRhoNat), but the correlation is near zero in inner London ($\nepiimdRhoInner$) and negative in Manchester ($\nepiimdRhoManchester$) and Bristol ($\nepiimdRhoBristol$), where the least deprived areas hold the highest-access cores and proximity already carries a price (Extended Data Fig.~4). Revaluing proximity without expanding its supply would price lower-income households out of the neighbourhoods the decarbonisation case favours, so planning for access requires expanding the supply of compact neighbourhoods as well as scoring the existing stock.

The remaining limitations are stated with the direction of their likely bias. First, travel energy counts car travel only, so public transport is missing from both sides: its energy is not charged to the compact areas whose residents use it most (a flat-type area holds \nepitransitAdv$\times$ the stops within walking distance), and its stops are not credited as destinations. Charging that energy to residents narrows the total gap only to \nepiptAssumedTotal$\times$ (Extended Data Table~5), and a fuller treatment would draw on the national public-transport accessibility indicators \citep{dftjts2021,verduzcotorres2024}. Second, the energy is residential consumption only, so the commercial energy behind the amenities is charged to no neighbourhood; it is concentrated in the compact areas that host those amenities and serves the dispersed residents who drive to them, again against the reported gap. Third, these data measure compact form rather than mixed use; a jobs-housing balance measure leaves the rate unchanged at the interpretable scale (\nepimixBalanceRate$\times$ at MSOA, against \nepimixBalanceRateOa$\times$ at Output Area scale, where area size dominates), so the mixed-use element of the recommendation rests on the planning literature. Fourth, the estimand is place-level and the analysis ecological \citep{robinson1950,greenland2001}; the definitive self-selection test, with movers in panel microdata, is out of scope. Fifth, the insulation scenario assumes the certificate potential-to-current ratio captures achievable fabric improvement. Sixth, amenity location is treated as exogenous, and the counts measure opportunity rather than a per-capita entitlement, though reachable jobs (\nepiwalkJobs$\times$) exceed reachable people (\nepiwalkPeople$\times$). Seventh, the neighbourhood finding does not settle the metropolitan general-equilibrium question, where relocation can offset densification gains \citep{gaigne2012}. Eighth, energy saved through efficiency is partly offset by rebound \citep{sorrell2007}, so dwelling-level gains need not scale to economy-wide reductions. The analysis covers England only, pending a harmonised Welsh deprivation measure.

The energy productivity principle with which the paper opened can now be read against the results. The prediction that compact form obtains more use from an equivalent unit of energy is confirmed at neighbourhood scale, and its size is measured. In the terms of the opening illustration, the dispersed neighbourhood is the desert, where an equivalent unit of energy streams through and yields a single use; the compact neighbourhood extracts \nepirate$\times$ the use from the same unit. These figures cover housing and access to land uses, the two uses these datasets measure, and a fuller account of the complexity Jacobs described would likely place the advantage of compact form higher still. The difference persists until places are rebuilt, and no technology on the current deployment pathway reduces it. Decarbonisation strategy should therefore place greater emphasis on compact urban form, and on the mixed-use development with which planning practice pairs it, though only the first of those is measured here.

\section*{Methods}

This section runs in the order of the analysis: the unit and the input data; the construction of energy, access, and the rate for each Output Area; the compositional model that turns the per-area quantities into flat-to-detached contrasts; the scenarios that recompute energy under each measure; and the robustness checks on the estimand.

\subsection*{Unit of analysis}

The unit is the 2021 Census Output Area, the smallest published census geography, with about 125 households and 300 residents. A flat-type neighbourhood holds \nepipopDensityFlat{} residents per hectare against \nepipopDensityDet{} in a detached-type one, a factor of \nepipopDensityRatio{} (dominant-type medians). Of England's 178,605 Output Areas, the \nepisampleN{} with usable metered energy (areas with more than ten residents and positive metered consumption) are analysed. Wales is excluded because the English Index of Multiple Deprivation, used as a control, has no directly comparable Welsh equivalent. All inputs are open data, processed to the Output Area on the British National Grid (EPSG:27700): Census 2021 topic tables (population, household size, dwelling-type shares, tenure, occupational class, car ownership, commute distance, workplace jobs); DESNZ sub-national gas and electricity statistics; domestic Energy Performance Certificates (floor area, build year, current and potential intensity, aggregated as area medians); OS Open Roads; amenity registers (Food Standards Agency, Get Information about Schools, NHS Organisation Data Service, OS Open Greenspace); the Indices of Deprivation 2025; DVLA vehicle licensing; National Travel Survey table NTS9904; and HadUK-Grid 1991--2020 heating-degree-days.

\subsection*{Energy}

Energy is delivered energy in kilowatt-hours per dwelling per year, not primary energy or carbon. The home component is metered: postcode-level gas and electricity, each supplied as a meter count and a mean per meter, are aggregated to the Output Area by meter-weighted means, summed, and divided by census households. Gas is weather-corrected. Both series are the domestic sector of the DESNZ statistics, but the two sectors are defined differently. Electricity is separated by meter profile class, the domestic sector comprising the non-half-hourly standard and Economy 7 profiles, with meters on those profiles reallocated to the commercial sector above 100,000 kWh per year. Gas carries no use-based flag: every meter consuming less than 73,200 kWh per year is classified as domestic, so small commercial premises remain in the domestic series. Such premises are concentrated in the compact areas that hold ground-floor commercial stock, and their consumption enters the numerator without adding to the household denominator, so the leakage acts against the reported form gradient. Metered energy is used because certificate ratings over-predict consumption, most severely for large detached dwellings, and would inflate the form gradient with model error \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}.

The travel component is the total car-travel energy of a location's residents, not the commute alone (about one sixth of car mileage). It is estimated by constrained disaggregation. NTS9904 gives measured car-driver miles per person by 2021 rural-urban class of residence, from about 2,500 miles per person per year (urban) to 5,200 (rural). Each class total is distributed across its Output Areas by an allocator combining cars per person (elasticity 1, the proportional headline) and commute distance (elasticity 0.30), constrained so that each class's population-weighted mean reproduces the survey figure exactly; only the within-class distribution is estimated, and both elasticities are swept in the sensitivity analysis. Miles are converted to energy at the local fleet intensity, \nepitravelIce{} kWh per mile (internal combustion) and \nepitravelEv{} (battery electric), blended by the DVLA battery-electric share. The intensities correspond to 0.58 and 0.20 kWh per vehicle-kilometre: fleet-average combustion consumption of about 6.5 litres per 100 km at 8.9 kWh per litre, and the real-world consumption of current battery-electric cars, consistent with the Department for Transport's energy-consumption and appraisal tables. Allocated per-person miles are multiplied by census household size to give miles per dwelling before conversion to energy, so household size is part of the per-dwelling travel contrast. Commute distance enters the allocator as the household-weighted mean of the census distance-band midpoints. Metered domestic electricity already includes home electric-vehicle charging, so the travel figure double-counts that overlap; at the current battery-electric fleet share (2--3\%) the effect is negligible. Public-transport mileage from the same survey table is disaggregated in the same way for the sensitivity reported in Extended Data Table~5, with the Census TS061 public-transport share of commuters as the within-class allocator; it does not enter the headline energy total.

\subsection*{Access}

Access is the count of opportunities reachable from an Output Area over the road network. The England network (OS Open Roads, about 3.6 million junctions) is built once into a routable graph (cityseer \citep{simons2023}), and counts are accumulated outwards from each Output Area's nearest node at every distance band from 1,600\,m to 25,600\,m. Amenities are six everyday destination types: general practitioners and pharmacies (NHS), schools (GIAS), places to eat and drink and grocery shops (FSA), and public parks and greenspace (Ordnance Survey); sources, record counts, and filters are given in Extended Data Table~1. Jobs are workplace jobs, each workplace weighted by its job count; people are the resident population. Each is read at 1,600\,m, at the area's own car catchment, and at 25,600\,m. Public-transport stops (NaPTAN bus stops and rail, metro, and tram access points) are counted separately as a straight-line total within 1,600\,m and reported as context; a stop is a means of reach rather than a destination, so stops do not enter the amenity basket.

\subsection*{The rate}

For each Output Area, the rate is the amenity count within the area's own car catchment divided by its car-travel energy. The catchment is the area's allocated annual car-driver distance per person divided by 370 trips per person per year, approximating the National Travel Survey car-driver trip rate (table NTS0303: 363 in 2023, about 380 before the pandemic). The resulting one-way distance is used as the network reach, bounded to [1,600\,m, 25,600\,m]. Recomputing the catchments at 300 and 440 trips per year gives access advantages of \nepitripLowAdv$\times$ and \nepitripHighAdv$\times$ against the headline \nepitripMidAdv$\times$, and rates of \nepitripLowRate$\times$ and \nepitripHighRate$\times$ against \nepitripMidRate$\times$, so the level of the constant has little effect on the contrast. The flat-to-detached contrast of the rate is by construction the product of the access advantage (catchment amenities, flat:detached) and the energy saving (car-travel energy, detached:flat); it is reconstructable from Table~\ref{tab:axes}, not estimated as a separate model. The two terms are deliberately not independent: the catchment radius and the travel energy are both functions of how far residents drive, and this dependence is intended. The conditioning variants reported in the Results bound the rate's dependence on that construction.

\subsection*{The compositional model}

All contrasts derive from one compositional, no-intercept regression on each Output Area's dwelling-type shares (flat, terraced, semi-detached, detached, residual) as fractions summing to one. Without an intercept, each coefficient is the modelled outcome of a neighbourhood composed entirely of one type, and the ratio is $\exp(b_{\mathrm{detached}} - b_{\mathrm{flat}})$; the confounds enter uncentred and shift both pure-type predictions equally, so the ratio does not depend on the levels at which they are held. Regressions are household-weighted. The estimate is read at pure-composition vertices, which few areas approach, so each ratio states the contrast between idealised extremes. Support-respecting median counterparts are reported alongside: the ratio of medians between areas whose plurality dwelling type is flat and those whose plurality type is detached.

Energy enters in logarithms per dwelling, so fitted ratios are ratios of conditional geometric means. Household size and floor area are covariates with freely estimated coefficients, not denominators. The bivariate floor-area elasticity of home energy is \nepifloorElast{} and the within-model household-size elasticity is \nepihhElast, both well below one, so a fixed per-person or per-square-metre denominator would impose an elasticity the data reject. On the log scale, size and occupancy account for \nepimedShare\% (\nepimedShareCI\%) of the home-energy gap, a descriptive covariate adjustment rather than an identified mediation. Four confounds are held throughout: building age, deprivation (the overall Index of Multiple Deprivation and its income domain), tenure (social- and private-rented shares), and climate (heating-degree-days). Access counts are non-negative with frequent zeros and are fitted with a Poisson log link; with households as analytic weights the estimator is quasi-Poisson, using point estimates with a robust sandwich covariance. The access regression holds the income domain; it excludes density, which is the mechanism under study, and the overall deprivation index, whose barriers and living-environment sub-domains are themselves access measures.

Standard errors are clustered on the \nepiclusters{} local-authority districts, with $t(G-1)$ critical values; every reported ratio carries a 95\% interval on this basis. Districts are the clustering unit because areas within one share weather, stock history, and local policy. To check that districts are large enough to contain the spatial dependence, the total-gap interval was re-estimated with the districts merged into fifty contiguous spatial blocks (k-means on district centroids); it widens to \nepispatialBlockGapCI with the estimate unchanged, and the district clustering is retained. Composite quantities (the rate, the insulation-plus-electrification surviving-gap share, the mediated fraction) carry cluster-bootstrap intervals with 999 replications, resampling whole districts; the per-scenario ratios carry their own clustered intervals (Extended Data Table~2), from which the scenario closure shares derive.

\subsection*{Decarbonisation scenarios}

Energy is recomputed under the technology measures and the gap re-estimated. Insulation scales each home's metered gas by its certificate fabric-improvement ratio (modelled potential over current intensity, median \nepifabricFactorMedian, clipped to the unit interval; both terms are modelled, so the performance gap cancels); electricity is unchanged. Heat pumps deliver the gas heat as electricity at the delivered-energy factor of boiler efficiency over a seasonal coefficient of performance ($0.90/2.8 = 0.32$); a sensitivity analysis varies the coefficient between 2.4 and 3.2. Electrification recomputes car energy at electric fleet intensity with mileage unchanged. Both heat measures act on metered gas only, so electrically heated homes, more common among flats, receive no retrofit measure; this bias is conservative for the surviving-gap shares. Partial uptake blends transformed and untransformed energy linearly; the Balanced Pathway scenario uses the Climate Change Committee's 2040 stock shares \citep{ccc2025}. Every scenario is fitted on one common complete-case sample (\nepiscenarioN{} areas with a valid fabric ratio). Access is unchanged in every scenario, since no measure moves a destination.

\subsection*{Robustness and the estimand}

The estimand is place-level: the energy and access profile of a neighbourhood type conditional on observed confounds, not a household treatment effect. Three checks bound selection on unobservables. Access depends on location alone and is therefore unaffected by sorting. Occupational class (higher managerial and professional share) is added to the confounds and leaves the gaps essentially unchanged. A coefficient-stability bound \citep{oster2019} is computed on a continuous detached-share gradient in an intercept specification (the compositional model has no centred $R^2$), using the coefficient-based approximation with $R_{\max} = \min(1.3\tilde{R}, 1)$; the Results report $\delta^{*}$ together with the $\delta = 1$ bias-adjusted gap. The zonation check re-aggregates all variables, household-weighted, to LSOA and MSOA geographies and re-fits the identical model.
