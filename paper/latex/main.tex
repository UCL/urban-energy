\abstract{Decarbonisation policy reduces the energy that homes and vehicles consume, through insulation, heat pumps, and electric vehicles. How much energy a neighbourhood needs, however, is also set by its form: compact neighbourhoods lose less heat through shared walls, and their residents drive less because destinations lie nearer. A neighbourhood's form outlives these measures, and the question is whether they can counteract the energy premium of dispersed form, or whether compact form deserves attention in its own right. We compare neighbourhoods across England's full range of dwelling mix, from flats to detached houses, on the energy their homes and cars consume and the access their residents obtain. Energy comes from meters and from the National Travel Survey, and access from counts over the road network, for \nepisampleN{} Census 2021 Output Areas, with contrasts read between idealised pure types. On foot, a flat neighbourhood reaches about \nepiwalkAmenRound{} times the amenities of a detached one; matching the count by driving costs the detached neighbourhood \nepitravelGap{} times the car-travel energy, and \nepitotalGapRound{} times the total energy per dwelling with home energy included. Per unit of car-travel energy, the flat neighbourhood obtains \nepirate{} times the access. Applying the three measures at the uptakes of the national pathway, and then in full, narrows the energy gap only part way. With every home insulated to its certificate potential and every car electric, the detached neighbourhood still spends \nepilockinGap{} times the energy and still reaches the same amenities only by driving. For new development, the results argue for urgent emphasis on compact form as a lever for reducing energy, to avoid locking in significant future demand.}

\keywords{urban form, accessibility, energy, decarbonisation, compact city, neighbourhood}

Energy and decarbonisation policy evaluates progress by the energy that homes and vehicles consume and the carbon they emit, and it targets the same assets with insulation, heat pumps, and electric vehicles \citep{ukgovernment2023,ccc2025}. The energy those homes and vehicles need, however, also depends on how cities are arranged. Dwellings in a more compact neighbourhood share more of their building envelope, so they lose less heat. Destinations also lie nearer, which reduces car use and the energy spent on travel. The morphology behind that demand receives little attention in energy policy. This approach may well suit the existing stock, whose form is already fixed, therefore requiring emphasis on demand-side measures; however, it risks leaving a blind spot in new urban development, where---even with insulation, heat pumps, and electric vehicles fully deployed---what is built now may exact an energy premium. Such a premium would be locked in, because only rebuilding could remove it and the embodied carbon of demolition and construction precludes rebuilding. The question is therefore whether these demand-side measures are sufficient to counteract the energy premium of dispersed urban form, or whether compact form deserves attention in its own right. We show that across \nepisampleN{} English neighbourhoods a dispersed neighbourhood spends about \nepitotalGapRound$\times$ the energy of a compact one, heating larger homes and driving further to reach a comparable count of everyday amenities. With every home insulated to its certificate potential and every car electric, it still spends \nepilockinGap$\times$ the energy. The premium that survives is locked into the form of the neighbourhood. Compact form therefore warrants greater weight in energy policy, above all in new development.

The premise behind the study is that compact urban form affords higher energy productivity than dispersed urban form. This is inspired by Jane Jacobs's idea of ``energy in the conduit'' \citep{jacobs2000}: the greater the compactness, complexity, and diversity of a system, the more use it obtains from an equivalent unit of energy. The greater the intensity and complexity of a system's interdependencies, the more it is capable of harnessing and repeatedly recycling a unit of energy through the system before it is lost, therefore doing more with less, or, in another sense, getting additional use out of the same amount of energy ``for free''. Jacobs illustrated the idea with a rainforest and a desert: the rainforest folds a unit of incoming energy through many organisms and close exchanges, extracting use from it repeatedly before it dissipates. The desert, for an equivalent unit of energy, does substantially less, because the energy streams through and is lost without entering such exchanges. She applied the framing to economies \citep{jacobs1969,jacobs2000}, and the themes of her urban thinking, such as density, functional diversity, and interconnectedness, follow from the same principle \citep{jacobs1961}. Applied to cities, this notion implies that energy productivity \citep{patterson1996} should rise with urban compactness. Because cities are complex, the full implications of her account resist comprehensive measurement, but a related regularity has been quantified in the urban scaling literature. As cities grow, incomes, productivity, and innovation rise faster than population does, while infrastructure and service delivery require less per person \citep{bettencourt2007,glaeser2011}. Those results concern the size of cities rather than their form, but the same underlying concept is extrapolated here. At neighbourhood scale the principle shows in two ways. A given expenditure of energy houses more households, and those households obtain more access in return. We ask, from the perspective of domestic energy policy, whether both hold across England's morphological gradient from flats to detached houses, with each area's dwelling-type composition standing for its compactness. The first is measured as the total energy a neighbourhood spends to house a household, the second as the access gained per unit of the energy spent on travel, hereafter the rate.

The policy instruments in current use are aimed instead at individual assets: fabric standards and retrofit subsidy keyed to the energy certificate, heat-pump deployment targets, and vehicle electrification mandates \citep{ukgovernment2023,ccc2025}. These instruments are usually criticised for accuracy, since certificate ratings routinely over-predict the energy that homes use \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,sunikka-blank2012}. The criticism made here concerns what the instruments measure rather than their accuracy. On the building side, a certificate rates a dwelling against its own fabric and services, per square metre of floor area. A compact home and a dispersed one can therefore carry the same rating while consuming different amounts of energy, because the rating is normalised by floor area and the dispersed home is larger. On the travel side, dispersed urban form sets the distances residents drive, yet policy focuses mainly on vehicle efficiency rather than on those distances. Compact development reduces driving \citep{trb2009,ewing2018}, and local amenity supply is the primary determinant of trip-making \citep{abbiasov2024}. Cities where more destinations lie within a walk emit less transport carbon per person \citep{marzolla2025}. Modelling suggests that electrifying cars without reducing driving cuts emissions too slowly to stay within carbon budgets \citep{winkler2023}. Planning has long attended to neighbourhood form, from the critique of automobile dependence to compact cities and the fifteen-minute city \citep{newman1999,rode2017,moreno2021}, but its metrics are chiefly counts and travel times. Neither approach conventionally states what a neighbourhood's compactness is worth in energy.

We combine the planning literature's attention to compact urban form with the energy metrics of decarbonisation policy. For the whole of England, at neighbourhood resolution, we evaluate the dwelling mix across its full spectrum, from flats through terraced and semi-detached houses to detached ones. For each neighbourhood we measure the energy its homes and its cars consume and the access its residents obtain, both taken from measured records rather than from models. Together they express, in Jacobs's terms, the use each neighbourhood obtains from the energy it spends. We then apply the demand-side measures in full, insulating every home and electrifying every car, and read the energy gap that remains between compact and dispersed forms as the lock-in.

Each half of this design has a precedent in earlier work. The first, in transport and accessibility research, developed the idea of valuing access by what it costs to obtain. Hou and colleagues constructed the mobility energy productivity metric to measure the quality of mobility a United States location affords \citep{hou2019}. It counts the opportunities reachable from a place, weighted by the time, money, and energy required. Kinigadner and colleagues inverted the budget, asking how many destinations can be reached within a fixed emission allowance \citep{kinigadner2020}. In full cost accessibility, Cui and Levinson brought crash risk and emissions into the monetised travel cost against which accessibility is computed \citep{cui2018}. All three are appraisal tools for locations and proposals, and their energy terms are modelled rather than measured.

The second, in energy research, measures what households consume. Chatterton and colleagues mapped direct energy use across the 34,753 Lower-layer Super Output Areas of England and Wales, neighbourhoods averaging 1,600 residents, joining metered gas and electricity with car energy from odometer readings \citep{chatterton2016}. Their purpose was distributional: a minority of areas consume far more than the rest, and carbon policy, they argued, should attend to high consumers as well as the energy poor. They also arrived at this paper's question from the energy side, asking whether high consumption reflects need or choice, with housing type and poor accessibility among the candidates, and framing the constrained case as lock-in. Answering that question requires a measure of access, which their record does not contain, and a finer unit, because compact and dispersed forms interleave within a single town. The Output Areas used here sit one census level below their Super Output Areas, typically five to each; at about 125 households, they are small enough for a single dwelling type to dominate.

The energy and access measured for each neighbourhood are fitted on its dwelling-type mix, holding the confounds constant. Both gaps are read between idealised pure types, from all flats to all detached houses. The rate is formed from the access and the travel component of the energy rather than fitted separately. We then ask how much of the gap the demand-side measures can close. Each neighbourhood's energy is recomputed with insulation, heat pumps, and electric vehicles applied separately, at the uptakes of England's advised pathway and at full deployment \citep{ccc2025}.

The comparison between compact and dispersed forms is subject to three hazards. The first is how home energy is measured. Modelled energy exaggerates the contrast between forms: simulation places the heat-demand gap between detached and compact archetypes at up to sixfold \citep{rode2014}, while metered analyses put detached gas consumption at about twice that of a flat \citep{need2024,ehs2024,wyatt2013}. The exaggeration arises because certificate ratings are modelled and noisily assessed \citep{crawley2019}, and they over-predict consumption most for the largest dwellings \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}. Home energy therefore comes from meters. The second hazard is inferring travel from density. Density is associated with lower car use \citep{newman1989}, but the effect is modest \citep{ewing2017,echenique2012}, contested at the metropolitan scale \citep{gaigne2012,mindali2004}, and better read as one of accessibility \citep{ewing2018,ellder2022}. Travel energy therefore comes from measured national mileage, with the allocation assumptions swept in the robustness analysis, and access is counted over the road network rather than proxied by density.

The third hazard is the choice of denominator. Energy per person, per dwelling, and per square metre give different verdicts: a low-density premium that is large per capita narrows or reverses per square metre \citep{norman2006}. Two people sharing a home use less than twice the energy of one \citep{druckman2008,huebner2017}, and a large home spreads its heating over more floor area, so dividing by persons or by square metres rewards the larger household or the larger home. Energy therefore enters per dwelling, and household size and floor area enter as controls, with their effects estimated from the data rather than fixed by the choice of denominator. A final caution concerns who lives where. Households choose their dwellings \citep{cao2009,stevens2017}, so the comparison is between places rather than between households, describing a neighbourhood type conditional on the observed confounds. Its exposure to residential self-selection is bounded with a coefficient-stability test. Figure~\ref{fig:inversion} shows that comparison, setting energy against access for the four dwelling types.

\section*{Results}

Every flat-to-detached contrast below derives from one compositional, no-intercept regression on each Output Area's dwelling-type mix, household-weighted, holding building age, deprivation, tenure, and local climate constant (Methods). The sample is \nepisampleN{} of England's 178,605 Census 2021 Output Areas. The contrast is read between idealised pure types, so it states the upper end of each gap; the gap between typical mixed areas is smaller, and both figures are reported. Confidence intervals are 95\%, clustered on \nepiclusters{} local-authority districts. The argument rests on access and the rate; the energy gap enters as the price of the access deficit rather than as an independent causal claim.

\subsection*{Access}

Access is the count of opportunities reachable from an area over the road network: amenities (six everyday destination types), jobs (each workplace weighted by its job count), and people (resident population). Each count is read at a short walk (1,600\,m, about twenty minutes on foot), at the area's own car catchment, and at a long drive (25,600\,m) (Methods). Access is a property of the location rather than of its residents, so it does not depend on how the household is counted, and it is, by construction, unaffected by residential self-selection.

The access gap is largest at walking distance and remains open at every distance examined (Table~\ref{tab:axes}, Fig.~\ref{fig:reach}). On foot, a flat-type neighbourhood reaches \nepiwalkAmen$\times$ (95\% CI \nepiwalkAmenCI) the amenities, \nepiwalkJobs$\times$ (\nepiwalkJobsCI) the jobs, and \nepiwalkPeople$\times$ (\nepiwalkPeopleCI) the people of a detached-type one. Between typical areas the on-foot amenity gap is \nepiwalkAmenDomMed$\times$ comparing dominant-type medians and \nepiwalkAmenSupport$\times$ comparing areas that are at least half of one type. Per-service counts at walking distance are given in Extended Data Fig.~2. At each area's own car catchment the raw counts nearly converge (amenities \nepicatchAmen$\times$, \nepicatchAmenCI), because a detached area's residents drive further, into a larger catchment. At a 25.6\,km drive, where a detached area's residents can reach denser places, the flat-type area still reaches \nepidriveAmen$\times$ the amenities, \nepidriveJobs$\times$ the jobs, and \nepidrivePeople$\times$ the people. For context, a flat-type neighbourhood holds \nepipopDensityFlat{} residents per hectare, compared with \nepipopDensityDet{} in a detached-type one, a factor of \nepipopDensityRatio{} (dominant-type medians). Public-transport access follows the same gradient: a flat-type neighbourhood has \nepitransitAdv$\times$ (\nepitransitAdvCI) the bus and rail stops within 1,600\,m (straight-line count; Methods). Stops are a means of reach rather than destinations, so this gradient is not credited in the access counts, and the public-transport travel that accompanies it is not charged to the energy totals either (Extended Data Table~5). The deficit at walking distance therefore closes only by driving.

\subsection*{Energy}

Energy is the cost side of the comparison: the driving through which the access deficit is closed, together with the home consumption that dispersed form raises alongside it. Total energy is delivered energy per dwelling per year, in two measured components: the home's metered gas and electricity, and the car-travel energy of its residents. The home component is metered rather than modelled, which avoids the performance gap; the travel component is estimated by disaggregating measured National Travel Survey mileage across areas under exact class constraints (Methods).

Total energy per dwelling in a detached neighbourhood is \nepitotalGap$\times$ (95\% CI \nepitotalGapCI) that of a flat-type one, and \nepifamNowGap$\times$ at equal household size (Table~\ref{tab:axes}). The gradient is steepest in travel, \nepitravelGap$\times$ (\nepitravelGapCI); the metered home component is \nepiheatGap$\times$ (\nepiheatGapCI). The home-energy gap combines three consequences of low density: larger dwellings, larger households, and a more exposed envelope. Holding household size equal moves it to \nepiheatFamGap$\times$ (\nepiheatFamGapCI); holding floor area equal as well leaves \nepiheatSizeGap$\times$ (\nepiheatSizeGapCI), the direct effect of exposed walls and unshared surfaces. On the log scale, size and occupancy account for \nepimedShare\% (\nepimedShareCI\%) of the home-energy gap; this is a descriptive covariate adjustment rather than an identified mediation. The bivariate elasticity of home energy with respect to floor area is \nepifloorElast, and the household-size elasticity estimated within the model is \nepihhElast. Both are well below one, so no fixed denominator is used: dividing by residents or by floor area would impose an elasticity of one that the data reject.

Access and energy can now be placed in space. Figure~\ref{fig:map} maps them side by side. By energy, dispersed high-consumption form covers most of England's land area and the compact cities form a low-consumption minority. By walkable access, the pattern reverses: high access is confined almost entirely to those same cities. The corresponding contrast within a single city, between a compact core and a dispersed edge, is shown in Extended Data Fig.~3, and the joint distribution over all individual areas in Extended Data Fig.~1.

\subsection*{The rate: access per kilowatt-hour}

The near-convergence of catchment counts is the reason access and energy require joint measurement: a count taken at each area's own car catchment shows the access gap as nearly closed, and the energy identifies the additional driving through which the closure is achieved. The rate joins the two quantities: it is the amenity count within the area's own car catchment divided by the area's car-travel energy, and car energy is the denominator because it is the energy through which access beyond walking distance is obtained. At their own catchments the two forms reach a similar count (access advantage \nepicatchAmen$\times$), but the detached area does so with about three times the car energy (energy saving \nepitravelGap$\times$). Per kilowatt-hour, the flat-type area therefore obtains $\nepicatchAmen \times \nepitravelGap \approx \nepirate\times$ (95\% CI \nepirateCI) the access (Fig.~\ref{fig:rate}).

The value depends on how the two components are conditioned, and this dependence is reported. Matching the access side's income-only conditioning on the energy side raises the rate to \nepirateHarm$\times$ (\nepirateHarmCI). Computing the rate against already-electrified car-travel energy, which removes the dependence between driving distance and catchment size, lowers it to \nepirateCirc$\times$ (\nepirateCircCI); the dominant-type median rate is \nepirateDomMedian$\times$. The headline \nepirate$\times$ lies within this range, and the ordering is preserved under every variant considered.

\subsection*{Decarbonisation scenarios and lock-in}

The rate can change only if technology moves access or energy, and the technologies on the national pathway all act on energy. The scenario ladder recomputes energy under the principal technology measures of the strategy, alone and in combination (Methods); access is unchanged in every scenario, because none of these technologies alters the distance between dwellings and destinations, and the on-foot amenity contrast remains \nepiwalkAmenRound$\times$ throughout (Fig.~\ref{fig:scenarios}). Every scenario reduces absolute consumption in every neighbourhood, but the three measures act differently on the gap between forms. Insulation scales each home's gas by its own certificate-derived fabric headroom, which is larger for detached homes, and closes more of the gap than any other single measure: fully deployed, it moves the total gap from \nepiscenarioBase$\times$ to \nepifabricGap$\times$, closing \nepifabricClosed\% of the log gap. Electrification recomputes car mileage at electric energy intensity and closes \nepievClosed\% of the log gap, leaving \nepievGap$\times$. Heat pumps reduce delivered home energy sharply but near-uniformly, which raises the relative weight of the larger travel gap; fully deployed they leave the total gap marginally wider, at \nepihpGap$\times$.

On the Climate Change Committee's Balanced Pathway for 2040, with half of homes on heat pumps and three-quarters of cars electric \citep{ccc2025}, the gap is \nepicccGap$\times$, with \nepicccClosed\% closed. This row corresponds to the pathway advised for the legislated carbon budgets, so the ladder evaluates the composition of the national strategy rather than a hypothetical programme. Full deployment of all three measures leaves the total gap at \nepifullGap$\times$, with \nepifullSurvives\% of the log gap surviving. Confidence intervals for the ladder scenarios are given in Extended Data Table~2.

The headline lock-in bound is read at fully deployed insulation plus vehicle electrification, without heat pumps: heat pumps switch the fuel of delivered heat rather than reduce the demand for it and thereby widen the gap, so excluding them grants the technology pathway its most favourable case. Under that bound the total gap is \nepilockinGap$\times$ (\nepilockinGapCI), with \nepisurvivesShare\% (\nepisurvivesShareCI\%) of the log gap surviving; at equal household size the same measures move \nepifamNowGap$\times$ to \nepifamOptGap$\times$. The access deficit of dispersed form is untouched by any scenario, because it is fixed in the street layout and the location of destinations, which change over generations rather than over technology-replacement cycles.

\subsection*{Robustness}

Figure~\ref{fig:forest} presents every headline ratio with its clustered confidence interval, and the estimates are examined against three threats to validity.

The first concerns residential self-selection: households who choose detached homes may differ in unmeasured ways that also raise energy use, so the energy gaps are read as place-level profiles rather than household treatment effects. Adding occupational class to the confounds leaves the gaps essentially unchanged. A coefficient-stability bound \citep{oster2019} on a continuous detached-share specification gives $\delta^{*} \approx \nepiosterDeltaTotal$ for total energy, indicating that unobserved sorting would need to be slightly stronger than all measured confounds combined to nullify the gap. The bound indicates survival at the margin rather than insensitivity: at $\delta = 1$ the adjusted gap is \nepiosterBetaAdjTotal$\times$, and the home-energy component is weaker still ($\delta^{*} \approx \nepiosterDeltaHeat$; Extended Data Table~3). This is why the claim hierarchy at the head of the Results places access and the rate first.

The second concerns the travel construction: because each rural-urban class total is anchored exactly to the measured survey figure, the two allocator assumptions only redistribute miles within a class. The commute-distance elasticity has little effect: varying it from 0.15 to 0.45 varies the travel contrast between \nepicommuteSweepLo$\times$ and \nepicommuteSweepHi$\times$ on dominant-type medians. The ownership elasticity is the more consequential assumption and is swept to its limiting value: the compositional travel gap is \nepitravelGap$\times$ under proportional allocation, \nepialphaEightGap$\times$ at an elasticity of 0.8, \nepialphaSixGap$\times$ at 0.6, and \nepialphaZeroGap$\times$ (\nepialphaZeroGapCI) at zero. At zero the ownership signal is removed; the between-class mileage gradient, the commute-distance term, and household size remain. Under any ownership allocation the travel contrast remains at or above about two and a half times (95\% CI at the zero-elasticity limit \nepialphaZeroGapCI). At equal household size the travel gap is \nepitravelFamGap$\times$ (\nepitravelFamGapCI), so, unlike the home-energy gap, it is not primarily a household-size effect. The travel energy counts car travel only, and adding public transport on the same construction, its mileage taken from the same survey table and disaggregated by the same constrained method, leaves the total gap at \nepiptAssumedTotal$\times$ (\nepiptAssumedTotalCI) at an assumed \nepiptIntensityAssumed$\times$ the car intensity per passenger-mile, and at \nepiptCeilingTotal$\times$ (\nepiptCeilingTotalCI) when every passenger-mile is charged at the car intensity itself (Extended Data Table~5).

The third concerns measurement and zonation: flat-type areas have fewer domestic gas meters than households (\nepimeterFlat{} per household against \nepimeterDet{} for detached), largely because all-electric flats heat with electricity, which the total captures; communal heating metered as non-domestic is a genuine but smaller undercount. The home-energy gap holds under the corresponding denominator restrictions: restricting the sample to areas with gas-meter coverage of at least 0.9 leaves it at \nepigasCovHeat$\times$, the electricity-meter restriction leaves \nepielecHeat$\times$, and winsorisation leaves \nepiwinsorHeat$\times$, so the measurement issue, to the extent that it biases the estimate, slightly understates the gap. The gas-coverage restriction lowers the total gap to \nepigasCovTotal$\times$, a composition effect of the subsample, which excludes the off-gas rural detached areas with the highest travel energy. Re-aggregating all variables to the two coarser census geographies and re-fitting the same model leaves the total gap at \nepimaupTotalOa$\times$, \nepimaupTotalLsoa$\times$, and \nepimaupTotalMsoa$\times$ at Output Area, LSOA, and MSOA scale. The support-respecting median counterpart is \nepimaupMedOa$\times$, \nepimaupMedLsoa$\times$, and \nepimaupMedMsoa$\times$, so the result is not an artefact of the zonation. The home-energy vertex contrast attenuates towards parity at MSOA scale, where no unit approaches a pure composition, while the total gap, on which the argument rests, holds at every scale (Extended Data Table~4).

\section*{Discussion}

The component results agree with the published estimates where they overlap; the contribution therefore lies in the combination rather than in any single component. The metered home-energy gap of \nepiheatGap$\times$ agrees with national consumption statistics \citep{need2024,ehs2024} and dwelling-level metered regressions \citep{wyatt2013}, and lies well below simulation contrasts \citep{rode2014}, a difference attributable to the performance gap \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}. The dependence of the conclusion on the functional unit reproduces lifecycle results \citep{norman2006}, and the below-one household-size elasticity is consistent with the downsizing and household-composition literature \citep{huebner2017,druckman2008}. The travel contrast agrees in direction with the urban-form literature but exceeds marginal-effect estimates \citep{echenique2012} because it is a different estimand: a cross-sectional contrast between the extremes of the form distribution, read against the observed rural-urban mileage gradient. The access measurement operationalises the accessibility interpretation of the density-travel relationship \citep{ewing2018,ellder2022}. The closest antecedents of the rate, the mobility energy productivity metric \citep{hou2019} and carbon-based accessibility \citep{kinigadner2020}, are planning scores built on modelled travel times, energy intensities, and emission factors; the rate here is an empirical national contrast on measured energy, read between dwelling forms and evaluated under decarbonisation scenarios. The neighbourhood-scale finding does not settle the metropolitan general-equilibrium question, in which relocation responses can offset densification gains \citep{gaigne2012}.

The scenario results identify the mechanism: uniform technology scales energy down without changing the structural quantities. Insulation lowers heat loss per square metre but leaves floor area unchanged, and electrification lowers energy per mile but leaves mileage unchanged. Full deployment of all three measures leaves \nepifullSurvives\% of the log gap; without heat pumps, insulation plus electrification leaves \nepisurvivesShare\%. Heat pumps, which reduce delivered home energy the most, leave the form gap marginally wider because the remainder is dominated by travel. The access deficit is unchanged in every scenario. This is lock-in in the strict sense \citep{unruh2000,seto2016}: the quantities that set the gap are fixed in built form and street layout for generations. For the whole technology set, the conclusion parallels what mobility-transition modelling finds for electrification alone: without demand reduction, vehicle-technology substitution is too slow for cumulative carbon budgets \citep{winkler2023}.

Whether a detached neighbourhood is more energy-intensive at all depends on the functional unit: per dwelling it consumes more, per person the gap narrows because detached homes hold larger households, and per square metre it narrows further and slightly reverses (\nepimSqGap$\times$). The specification adopted here lets the data determine the scaling instead of a fixed denominator (Methods). The rate is the quantity least exposed to the choice. Its access term is a property of the location, for which no per-person variant exists, and its energy term is the travel gap, which moves only from \nepitravelGap$\times$ to \nepitravelFamGap$\times$ at equal household size, so the access-per-kilowatt-hour conclusion does not depend on the functional unit.

For policy, the results identify two asymmetries in the current instrument set. First, the instruments address the smaller of the two energy gaps. The asset-level instruments (certificates, fabric subsidy, and boiler replacement) operate on home energy, where the form gap is \nepiheatGap$\times$ and falls to \nepiheatSizeGap$\times$ at equal household size and floor area. The travel gap, \nepitravelGap$\times$ and robust to every ownership-allocation assumption down to \nepialphaZeroGap$\times$, has no dedicated asset-level instrument, because it is generated by the neighbourhood's location rather than by an asset a household owns. Second, the largest available energy saving is not scored by any instrument in the current set. A compact location provides \nepirate$\times$ the access per kilowatt-hour of driving, a larger relative differential between forms than any single technology measure produces in energy. No certificate, subsidy, or planning metric in current use records this differential, so the option that improves both quantities is absent from that accounting. A consumption assessment also misreads the scenario results: it shows the national programme succeeding, since absolute energy falls in every neighbourhood, while the relative position of places and the access deficit underlying it remain essentially unchanged. The same restriction rewards low-density form for its low per-capita home energy while ignoring its travel and access penalties.

Evaluating access alongside energy addresses both asymmetries: retrofit and electrification cannot supply proximity, and the rate expresses proximity in the same unit as those measures. Decarbonising buildings and vehicles alone does not close the gap; access must be planned and scored alongside energy. We refer to the two quantities and the rate between them as the neighbourhood energy performance index (NEPI). One caveat applies to the energy side: energy saved through efficiency is partly offset by rebound \citep{sorrell2007}, so dwelling-level gains need not scale to economy-wide reductions.

Revaluing access also carries a distributional implication. Access is capitalised into land prices and rents \citep{alonso1964}, but the present distribution in England runs against that expectation. The median area in the most income-deprived decile reaches about \nepiimdAccessRatio$\times$ the walkable amenities of the least deprived decile, which in turn uses \nepiimdEnergyRatio$\times$ the energy, because deprived households are concentrated in compact form and affluent households in dispersed form. Compact access is not at present a premium good at national scale. Within the strongest housing markets, however, the gradient flattens or inverts: across England access rises with income deprivation (rank correlation \nepiimdRhoNat), but in inner London the correlation falls to effectively zero ($\nepiimdRhoInner$) and it turns negative in Manchester ($\nepiimdRhoManchester$) and Bristol ($\nepiimdRhoBristol$), where the least deprived areas hold the highest-access cores and proximity already carries a price (Extended Data Fig.~4). The inversion is not explained by the overall affluence of the city: Manchester ranks among the most income-deprived cities in the comparison, yet its accessible core is held by its least-deprived neighbourhoods. The distributional risk is therefore visible wherever demand is strongest. Revaluing proximity without expanding its supply would price lower-income households out of the neighbourhoods the decarbonisation case favours, so planning for access requires expanding the supply of compact neighbourhoods as well as scoring the existing stock.

Four limitations concern what the two measurements cover. First, the travel energy counts car travel only, so public transport is absent from both measurements in opposing directions: its energy is not charged to compact areas, whose residents travel further by bus and rail, and the stops that serve them are not credited as destinations in the access counts. The National Travel Survey bounds the energy omission, since it measures public-transport mileage by the same classes of residence as car mileage; only the energy per passenger-mile, which turns on vehicle load factors, has no measured counterpart. Charging that energy to residents leaves the total gap at \nepiptAssumedTotal$\times$ at an assumed \nepiptIntensityAssumed$\times$ the car intensity per passenger-mile, and at \nepiptCeilingTotal$\times$ (\nepiptCeilingTotalCI) even when every passenger-mile is priced as a mile driven alone in a petrol car (Extended Data Table~5). A fuller treatment of transport would draw on the national frequency-weighted public-transport accessibility indicators now available \citep{dftjts2021,verduzcotorres2024}. Second, the energy is confined to residential consumption, so it attributes to an area the metered domestic gas and electricity and the car travel of its residents rather than everything consumed within its boundary. Non-domestic load lies largely outside the metered series (Methods), so the commercial energy supporting the amenities that enter the access counts is charged to no neighbourhood, although it is concentrated in the compact areas that host those amenities and serves the residents of dispersed areas who drive to them. Third, these data measure compact form, not mixed use. Adding a jobs-housing balance measure, built from workplace jobs against resident households, to the same models leaves the rate unchanged at the scale where the measure is interpretable: a shift across the middle four-fifths of its distribution gives \nepimixBalanceRate$\times$ at Middle Layer Super Output Area scale, against \nepimixBalanceRateOa$\times$ at Output Area scale, where the measure is dominated by area size rather than by land use. The mixed-use element of the recommendation therefore rests on the planning literature rather than on this evidence; a land-use mix measure built from non-domestic floorspace by use class would be the test. Fourth, the analysis covers England only, pending a harmonised Welsh deprivation measure.

Five further limitations concern inference. First, the estimand is place-level, and the definitive self-selection test, which differences out fixed preferences with movers in panel microdata, is out of scope. Second, the analysis is ecological, at area rather than household level, with the usual inferential cautions \citep{robinson1950,greenland2001}. Third, the insulation measure assumes that the certificate potential-to-current ratio captures achievable fabric improvement. Fourth, the access counts treat amenity location as exogenous, though amenities partly follow demand; the near-convergence of counts at each area's own catchment is consistent with the compact advantage being one of proximity rather than of supply. Fifth, the access figures describe what residents can currently reach, and compact areas hold more residents per amenity, so the counts measure opportunity rather than a per-capita entitlement. The people gradient is reported so that the quotient can be formed: reachable jobs (\nepiwalkJobs$\times$) exceed reachable people (\nepiwalkPeople$\times$), so the jobs-per-resident contrast still favours compact form.

The Jacobs premise with which the paper opened can now be read against the results. The prediction that compact form obtains more use from an equivalent unit of energy is confirmed at neighbourhood scale, and its size is measured. Housing a household costs a dispersed neighbourhood \nepifamNowGap$\times$ the energy of a compact one at equal household size, and \nepifamOptGap$\times$ with every home at its certificate potential and every car electric. On foot, a compact neighbourhood reaches about \nepiwalkAmenRound$\times$ the amenities of a dispersed one. In the terms of the opening illustration, the dispersed neighbourhood is the desert, where an equivalent unit of energy streams through and yields a single use; the compact neighbourhood extracts \nepirate$\times$ the use from the same unit. These figures cover housing and access to land uses, the two uses these datasets measure. A fuller account of the complexity Jacobs described, beyond this field of application, would likely place the advantage of compact form higher still.

Assessed by the access gained per unit of the energy spent on travel, compact form is the more efficient of the two. The difference persists until places are rebuilt, and no technology on the current deployment pathway reduces it. We therefore argue that decarbonisation strategy should place greater emphasis on compact urban form, and on the mixed-use development with which planning practice pairs it, though only the first of those is measured here. Compact form acts on the structural lock-in that the technology measures leave in place, and it improves energy and access together.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Unit of analysis}

The unit is the 2021 Census Output Area, the smallest published census geography, with about 125 households and 300 residents. Of England's 178,605 Output Areas, the \nepisampleN{} with usable metered energy (areas with more than ten residents and positive metered consumption) are analysed. Wales is excluded because the English Index of Multiple Deprivation, used as a control, has no directly comparable Welsh equivalent. All inputs are open data, processed to the Output Area on the British National Grid (EPSG:27700): Census 2021 topic tables (population, household size, dwelling-type shares, tenure, occupational class, car ownership, commute distance, workplace jobs); DESNZ sub-national gas and electricity statistics; domestic Energy Performance Certificates (floor area, build year, current and potential intensity, aggregated as area medians); OS Open Roads; amenity registers (Food Standards Agency, Get Information about Schools, NHS Organisation Data Service, OS Open Greenspace); the Indices of Deprivation 2025; DVLA vehicle licensing; National Travel Survey table NTS9904; and HadUK-Grid 1991--2020 heating-degree-days.

\subsection*{Energy}

Energy is delivered energy in kilowatt-hours per dwelling per year, not primary energy or carbon. The home component is metered: postcode-level gas and electricity, each supplied as a meter count and a mean per meter, are aggregated to the Output Area by meter-weighted means, summed, and divided by census households. Gas is weather-corrected. Both series are the domestic sector of the DESNZ statistics, but the two sectors are defined differently. Electricity is separated by meter profile class, the domestic sector comprising the non-half-hourly standard and Economy 7 profiles, with meters on those profiles reallocated to the commercial sector above 100,000 kWh per year. Gas carries no use-based flag: every meter consuming less than 73,200 kWh per year is classified as domestic, so small commercial premises remain in the domestic series. Such premises are concentrated in the compact areas that hold ground-floor commercial stock, and their consumption enters the numerator without adding to the household denominator, so the leakage acts against the reported form gradient. Metered energy is used because certificate ratings over-predict consumption, most severely for large detached dwellings, and would inflate the form gradient with model error \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}.

The travel component is the total car-travel energy of a location's residents, not the commute alone (about one sixth of car mileage). It is estimated by constrained disaggregation. NTS9904 gives measured car-driver miles per person by 2021 rural-urban class of residence, from about 2,500 miles per person per year (urban) to 5,200 (rural). Each class total is distributed across its Output Areas by an allocator combining cars per person (elasticity 1, the proportional headline) and commute distance (elasticity 0.30), constrained so that each class's population-weighted mean reproduces the survey figure exactly; only the within-class distribution is estimated, and both elasticities are swept in the sensitivity analysis. Miles are converted to energy at the local fleet intensity, \nepitravelIce{} kWh per mile (internal combustion) and \nepitravelEv{} (battery electric), blended by the DVLA battery-electric share. The intensities correspond to 0.58 and 0.20 kWh per vehicle-kilometre: fleet-average combustion consumption of about 6.5 litres per 100 km at 8.9 kWh per litre, and the real-world consumption of current battery-electric cars, consistent with the Department for Transport's energy-consumption and appraisal tables. Allocated per-person miles are multiplied by census household size to give miles per dwelling before conversion to energy, so household size is part of the per-dwelling travel contrast. Commute distance enters the allocator as the household-weighted mean of the census distance-band midpoints. Metered domestic electricity already includes home electric-vehicle charging, so the travel figure double-counts that overlap; at the current battery-electric fleet share (2--3\%) the effect is negligible. Public-transport mileage from the same survey table is disaggregated in the same way for the sensitivity reported in Extended Data Table~5, with the Census TS061 public-transport share of commuters as the within-class allocator; it does not enter the headline energy total.

\subsection*{Access}

Access is the count of opportunities reachable from an Output Area over the road network. The England network (OS Open Roads, about 3.6 million junctions) is built once into a routable graph (cityseer \citep{simons2023}), and counts are accumulated outwards from each Output Area's nearest node at every distance band from 1,600\,m to 25,600\,m. Amenities are six everyday destination types: general practitioners and pharmacies (NHS), schools (GIAS), places to eat and drink and grocery shops (FSA), and public parks and greenspace (Ordnance Survey); sources, record counts, and filters are given in Extended Data Table~1. Jobs are workplace jobs, each workplace weighted by its job count; people are the resident population. Each is read at 1,600\,m, at the area's own car catchment, and at 25,600\,m. Public-transport stops (NaPTAN bus stops and rail, metro, and tram access points) are counted separately as a straight-line total within 1,600\,m and reported as context; a stop is a means of reach rather than a destination, so stops do not enter the amenity basket.

\subsection*{The rate}

For each Output Area, the rate is the amenity count within the area's own car catchment divided by its car-travel energy. The catchment is the area's allocated annual car-driver distance per person divided by 370 trips per person per year, approximating the National Travel Survey car-driver trip rate (table NTS0303: 363 in 2023, about 380 before the pandemic). The resulting one-way distance is used as the network reach, bounded to [1,600\,m, 25,600\,m]. Recomputing the catchments at 300 and 440 trips per year gives access advantages of \nepitripLowAdv$\times$ and \nepitripHighAdv$\times$ against the headline \nepitripMidAdv$\times$, and rates of \nepitripLowRate$\times$ and \nepitripHighRate$\times$ against \nepitripMidRate$\times$, so the level of the constant has little effect on the contrast. The flat-to-detached contrast of the rate is by construction the product of the access advantage (catchment amenities, flat:detached) and the energy saving (car-travel energy, detached:flat); it is reconstructable from Table~\ref{tab:axes}, not estimated as a separate model. The two terms are deliberately not independent: the catchment radius and the travel energy are both functions of how far residents drive, and this dependence is intended. The conditioning variants reported in the Results bound the rate's dependence on that construction.

\subsection*{The compositional model}

All contrasts derive from one compositional, no-intercept regression on each Output Area's dwelling-type shares (flat, terraced, semi-detached, detached, residual) as fractions summing to one. Without an intercept, each coefficient is the modelled outcome of a neighbourhood composed entirely of one type, and the ratio is $\exp(b_{\mathrm{detached}} - b_{\mathrm{flat}})$; the confounds enter uncentred and shift both pure-type predictions equally, so the ratio does not depend on the levels at which they are held. Regressions are household-weighted. The estimate is read at pure-composition vertices, which few areas approach, so each ratio states the contrast between idealised extremes. Support-respecting median counterparts are reported alongside: the ratio of medians between areas whose plurality dwelling type is flat and those whose plurality type is detached.

Energy enters in logarithms per dwelling, so fitted ratios are ratios of conditional geometric means. Household size and floor area are covariates with freely estimated coefficients, not denominators. Four confounds are held throughout: building age, deprivation (the overall Index of Multiple Deprivation and its income domain), tenure (social- and private-rented shares), and climate (heating-degree-days). Access counts are non-negative with frequent zeros and are fitted with a Poisson log link; with households as analytic weights the estimator is quasi-Poisson, using point estimates with a robust sandwich covariance. The access regression holds the income domain; it excludes density, which is the mechanism under study, and the overall deprivation index, whose barriers and living-environment sub-domains are themselves access measures.

Standard errors are clustered on the \nepiclusters{} local-authority districts, with $t(G-1)$ critical values; every reported ratio carries a 95\% interval on this basis. Composite quantities (the rate, the insulation-plus-electrification surviving-gap share, the mediated fraction) carry cluster-bootstrap intervals with 999 replications, resampling whole districts; the per-scenario ratios carry their own clustered intervals (Extended Data Table~2), from which the scenario closure shares derive.

\subsection*{Decarbonisation scenarios}

Energy is recomputed under the technology measures and the gap re-estimated. Insulation scales each home's metered gas by its certificate fabric-improvement ratio (modelled potential over current intensity, median \nepifabricFactorMedian, clipped to the unit interval; both terms are modelled, so the performance gap cancels); electricity is unchanged. Heat pumps deliver the gas heat as electricity at the delivered-energy factor of boiler efficiency over a seasonal coefficient of performance ($0.90/2.8 = 0.32$); a sensitivity analysis varies the coefficient between 2.4 and 3.2. Electrification recomputes car energy at electric fleet intensity with mileage unchanged. Both heat measures act on metered gas only, so electrically heated homes, more common among flats, receive no retrofit measure; this bias is conservative for the surviving-gap shares. Partial uptake blends transformed and untransformed energy linearly; the Balanced Pathway scenario uses the Climate Change Committee's 2040 stock shares \citep{ccc2025}. Every scenario is fitted on one common complete-case sample (\nepiscenarioN{} areas with a valid fabric ratio). Access is unchanged in every scenario, since no measure moves a destination.

\subsection*{Robustness and the estimand}

The estimand is place-level: the energy and access profile of a neighbourhood type conditional on observed confounds, not a household treatment effect. Three checks bound selection on unobservables. Access depends on location alone and is therefore unaffected by sorting. Occupational class (higher managerial and professional share) is added to the confounds and leaves the gaps essentially unchanged. A coefficient-stability bound \citep{oster2019} is computed on a continuous detached-share gradient in an intercept specification (the compositional model has no centred $R^2$), using the coefficient-based approximation with $R_{\max} = \min(1.3\tilde{R}, 1)$; the Results report $\delta^{*}$ together with the $\delta = 1$ bias-adjusted gap. The zonation check re-aggregates all variables, household-weighted, to LSOA and MSOA geographies and re-fits the identical model.
