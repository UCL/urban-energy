\abstract{Decarbonisation policy reduces the energy that homes and vehicles consume through measures such as insulation, heat pumps, and electric vehicles. How much energy a neighbourhood needs, however, also depends on its form: compact neighbourhoods lose less heat through shared walls, and their residents drive less because destinations lie nearer. Whether measures applied to individual homes and vehicles can negate the energy premium of dispersed form is an open question. We compare England's neighbourhoods across the full range of dwelling mix, from wholly flats to wholly detached, holding building age, deprivation, tenure, and local climate constant. The energy data come from metered gas and electricity and National Travel Survey car mileage across \nepisampleN{} Census 2021 Output Areas. The most dispersed spend \nepiheatGap{} times the home energy of the most compact and \nepitravelGap{} times the car travel, which comes to \nepitotalGapRound{} times in total per dwelling, or \nepifamNowGap{} times at equal household size. When the three measures are applied in full, consumption falls everywhere, but the gap remains at \nepifullGap{} times, or \nepifullFamGap{} when household sizes are held equal. Dispersed development therefore adds a notable energy premium that remains locked in for the life of the building stock. We recommend greater attention to compact urban form in energy policy, above all for new development.}

\keywords{urban form, accessibility, energy, decarbonisation, compact city, neighbourhood}

Energy and decarbonisation policy evaluates progress by the energy that homes and vehicles consume and the carbon they emit, and it targets the same assets with insulation, heat pumps, and electric vehicles \citep{ukgovernment2023,ccc2025}. The energy those homes and vehicles need, however, also depends on the form of the city around them. Dwellings in a more compact neighbourhood share more of their building envelope, so they lose less heat. Destinations also lie nearer, which reduces car use and the energy spent on travel. The morphology behind that demand receives little attention in energy policy. This approach may well suit the existing stock, whose form is already fixed, therefore requiring emphasis on upgrades to the buildings and vehicles themselves; however, it risks leaving a blind spot in new urban development, where---even with insulation, heat pumps, and electric vehicles fully deployed---what is built now may exact an energy premium that remains locked in for the life of the building stock. Whether these upgrades can offset enough of that premium to negate the effect of dispersed form is therefore an open question.

The conceptual premise behind that question is that compact urban form affords higher energy productivity than dispersed urban form. This is inspired by Jane Jacobs's account of ecosystems and economies as conduits for energy \citep{jacobs2000}: the more compact, complex, and diverse a system, the more capable it is of harnessing and recycling a given unit of energy before that unit is lost. It therefore obtains more use from the same amount of energy, in effect for free; we refer to this as the energy productivity principle. In Jacobs's illustration, the rainforest's complex web of interdependencies harnesses a unit of incoming energy and recycles it through successive cascades of species, extracting maximal use before the unit is lost from the system. The desert lacks that complexity, so it cannot recycle the unit and extracts far less use from an equivalent amount of energy. She applied the framing to economies \citep{jacobs1969,jacobs2000}, and her urban themes of density, functional diversity, and interconnectedness express a similar intuition \citep{jacobs1961}. Applied to cities, the principle implies that energy productivity \citep{patterson1996} should rise with compactness. The urban scaling literature quantifies a related regularity, in which incomes and innovation improve faster than population as cities grow, while infrastructure and services are delivered with less per person \citep{bettencourt2007,glaeser2011}. Scaling, however, concerns the size of cities, which tracks compactness only loosely, and its gains are counted per person or in monetary terms rather than in energy. It is an impossibly complex task to follow the recycling of energy through every cascading dependency of a city. However, at the neighbourhood scale, it becomes possible to disentangle the relationship between energy and compactness in a more limited sense. By evaluating the energy a given area spends on homes and travel, we can interpret Jacobs's energy productivity literally, with energy as its unit. In theory, a more compact neighbourhood should house more households for a given unit of energy, and those households should gain greater access to amenities and services. In that comparison, the dispersed neighbourhood is the desert, extracting less use from an equivalent amount of energy.

The energy productivity of a neighbourhood is not a quantity the policy instruments in current use are built to register. They are aimed at individual assets: fabric standards and retrofit subsidy keyed to the energy certificate, heat-pump deployment targets, and vehicle electrification mandates \citep{ukgovernment2023,ccc2025}. These instruments are usually criticised for accuracy, since certificate ratings routinely over-predict the energy that homes use \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,sunikka-blank2012}. We criticise instead what they measure. On the building side, a certificate rates a dwelling against its own fabric and services, per square metre of floor area. A compact home and a dispersed one can therefore carry the same rating while consuming different amounts of energy, because the rating is normalised by floor area and the dispersed home is larger. On the travel side, dispersed urban form sets the distances residents drive, yet policy focuses mainly on vehicle efficiency rather than on those distances. Compact development reduces driving \citep{trb2009,ewing2018}, and local amenity supply is the primary determinant of trip-making \citep{abbiasov2024}. Cities where more destinations lie within a walk emit less transport carbon per person \citep{marzolla2025}. Modelling suggests that electrifying cars without reducing driving cuts emissions too slowly to stay within carbon budgets \citep{winkler2023}. Planning has long attended to neighbourhood form, from the critique of automobile dependence to compact cities and the fifteen-minute city \citep{newman1999,rode2017,moreno2021}, but its metrics are chiefly counts and travel times. Neither approach conventionally states what a neighbourhood's compactness is worth in energy.

To state that worth, we combine the planning literature's attention to compact urban form with the energy metrics of decarbonisation policy. We compare neighbourhoods from flats through terraced and semi-detached houses to detached ones, for the whole of England at neighbourhood resolution, so that energy and access can be followed across the full range of compactness. Rather than relying on models, we measure both quantities for each neighbourhood from records: energy as the metered consumption of its homes plus the car travel of its residents, and access as the count of destinations within their reach. Together they express the use each neighbourhood obtains from the energy it spends. We then apply the three measures in full and read the premium that remains as the lock-in. Some premium will always remain, because a detached home keeps its larger floor area, its exposed walls, and its farther destinations under every measure. The question is what degree of cancellation would be sufficient: a remainder small enough for dispersed development to stay a viable choice in a decarbonised stock. We answer it from the spread within each type. No two neighbourhoods of the same kind use exactly the same energy; one flat-type area uses somewhat more than another through the ordinary differences of place, so each type occupies a band of values rather than a single point. Were the measures to shrink the gap between forms to less than the width of that band, a treated detached-type neighbourhood would become indistinguishable from an ordinary flat-type one, and form would have stopped mattering. Where the gap stays wider than the band, we count the premium as notable: the treated detached type has not approached or overlapped the range of the compact one.

The design has two precedents. Transport and accessibility research values access by what it costs to obtain: the mobility energy productivity metric counts the opportunities reachable from a location, weighted by the time, money, and energy required \citep{hou2019}, later variants count destinations within a fixed emission allowance \citep{kinigadner2020} or price emissions into the travel cost \citep{cui2018}, and all are appraisal tools whose energy terms are modelled rather than measured. Energy research measures what households consume: Chatterton and colleagues joined metered gas and electricity with odometer car energy across the Super Output Areas of England and Wales, and arrived at this paper's question from the energy side, asking whether high consumption reflects need or choice, with housing type and poor accessibility among the candidates and the constrained case framed as lock-in \citep{chatterton2016}. Answering that question requires a measure of access, which we contribute here, and a finer unit of analysis. We adopt the Output Area, one census level below theirs; at about 125 households, it is small enough for a single dwelling type to dominate.

The comparison between compact and dispersed forms is subject to four hazards. The first is how home energy is measured. Modelled energy exaggerates the contrast between forms: simulation places the heat-demand gap between detached and compact archetypes at up to sixfold \citep{rode2014}, while metered analyses put detached gas consumption at about twice that of a flat \citep{need2024,ehs2024,wyatt2013}. The exaggeration arises because certificate ratings are modelled and noisily assessed \citep{crawley2019}, and they over-predict consumption most for the largest dwellings \citep{summerfield2019,few2023,firth2024}. Home energy therefore comes from gas and electricity meters. The second hazard is inferring travel from density. Density is associated with lower car use \citep{newman1989}, but the effect is modest \citep{ewing2017,echenique2012}, contested at the metropolitan scale \citep{gaigne2012,mindali2004}, and better read as one of accessibility \citep{ewing2018,ellder2022}. Travel energy therefore comes from measured national mileage, with the allocation assumptions swept in the robustness analysis, and access is counted over the road network rather than proxied by density.

The third hazard is the choice of denominator. Energy per person, per dwelling, and per square metre give different verdicts: a low-density premium that is large per capita narrows or reverses per square metre \citep{norman2006}. Two people sharing a home use less than twice the energy of one \citep{druckman2008,huebner2017}, so a home counted per person looks efficient only because more people share it. A large home spreads its heating over more floor area, so a home counted per square metre looks efficient only because it is large. Detached houses hold the larger households and the larger homes, so both units flatter dispersed form. Earlier studies have either reported the competing units side by side \citep{norman2006} or held household size as a covariate \citep{huebner2017}. We take the covariate route as the fairer one. Energy enters per dwelling, and household size and floor area enter as controls, with their effects estimated from the data rather than fixed by the choice of denominator. The fourth hazard is who lives where. Households choose their dwellings \citep{cao2009,stevens2017}, so the comparison is between places rather than between households. It cannot say what a household would spend if it moved; it describes a neighbourhood type, with the observed differences in building age, deprivation, tenure, and climate held constant. In the robustness analysis we quantify how large an unrecorded difference would have to be to account for the gap. Figure~\ref{fig:inversion} shows that comparison, setting energy against access for the four dwelling types.

\section*{Results}

The comparison covers the \nepisampleN{} of England's 178,605 Census 2021 Output Areas with usable metered energy. Real neighbourhoods lie on a spectrum of housing mixtures between two pure kinds, one wholly flats and one wholly detached houses. Few areas sit at either end, so comparing observed areas would compare mixtures. We therefore use a regression that reflects the composition of each area rather than a label for its majority type: each Output Area enters as the fractions of its homes that are flats, terraced, semi-detached, or detached, and the prediction at a fraction of one is what a neighbourhood built wholly of that type would consume once building age, deprivation, tenure, and local climate are held constant (Methods). The pure-type ratios compare idealised extremes that few real areas match, so Table~\ref{tab:axes} reports alongside each one the median of dominant types, the same contrast between the median flat-dominant area and the median detached-dominant one, which holds nothing constant but comes from areas that exist. Because adjacent Output Areas resemble one another, sharing weather, housing stock, and local policy, we compute the 95\% confidence intervals at the coarser scale of the \nepiclusters{} local-authority districts, so an interval narrows only where a ratio repeats across genuinely different places.

\subsection*{Energy}

The policy instruments concentrate on home energy. Its metered gap between forms is \nepiheatGap$\times$ (95\% CI \nepiheatGapCI). The headline figure includes three parts: larger dwellings, larger households, and a more exposed envelope. The gap moves to \nepiheatFamGap$\times$ (\nepiheatFamGapCI) when household size is held equal, which removes the larger households, and to \nepiheatSizeGap$\times$ (\nepiheatSizeGapCI) when floor area is held equal as well, which removes the larger dwellings; we read what remains as largely the price of the unshared envelope.

The larger share of the difference lies in travel. The car-travel energy of a detached neighbourhood is \nepitravelGap$\times$ (\nepitravelGapCI) that of a flat-type one, anchored to the measured mileage of each settlement class (Methods). Unlike the home component, the contrast is not primarily a household-size effect: it remains \nepitravelFamGap$\times$ when household size is held equal. Together the components put total energy per dwelling at \nepitotalGap$\times$ (\nepitotalGapCI), or \nepifamNowGap$\times$ at equal household size (Table~\ref{tab:axes}). The total also reads the other way: the energy that houses one detached-type household houses about two flat-type households.

\subsection*{Access}

We next count the amenities, jobs, and people each neighbourhood can reach over the road network. The amenities are a benchmark basket of six everyday destination types, from general practitioners to grocery shops, counted identically for every neighbourhood in the country (Methods; Extended Data Table~1). A flat-type neighbourhood reaches \nepiwalkAmen$\times$ the amenities, \nepiwalkJobs$\times$ the jobs, and \nepiwalkPeople$\times$ the people of a detached-type one on foot, within 1,600\,m or a twenty-minute walk (95\% CIs in Fig.~\ref{fig:forest}; per-service counts in Extended Data Fig.~2). Dispersed, high-energy form covers most of England's land area and walkable access is confined to the compact cities (Fig.~\ref{fig:map}; Extended Data Figs.~1 and~3). The counts nearly converge at each type's own car catchment, the distance its residents actually drive (amenities \nepicatchAmen$\times$, \nepicatchAmenCI); residents of dispersed areas extend their driving until their access is roughly equivalent. Nothing in the construction forces this convergence; the distances come from the travel survey and the counts from the road network. The flat-type area still reaches \nepidriveAmen$\times$ the amenities, \nepidriveJobs$\times$ the jobs, and \nepidrivePeople$\times$ the people even at a 25.6\,km drive (Table~\ref{tab:axes}, Fig.~\ref{fig:reach}).

\subsection*{The rate: access per kilowatt-hour}

The near-equal access at each type's own car catchment costs the detached area about three times the car-travel energy of the flat-type one. The rate joins the two quantities into an energy productivity for places: the count of the amenity basket within the area's own car catchment, divided by the area's car-travel energy, the energy through which access beyond walking distance is obtained. Its factors are the near-equal counts (access advantage \nepicatchAmen$\times$) and the threefold car energy (energy saving \nepitravelGap$\times$); the flat-type area therefore obtains $\nepicatchAmen \times \nepitravelGap \approx \nepirate\times$ (95\% CI \nepirateCI) the access to the benchmark basket per kilowatt-hour (Fig.~\ref{fig:rate}). The rate is \nepirateCirc$\times$ (\nepirateCircCI) when the denominator is total energy, home plus electrified travel (Methods).

\subsection*{Decarbonisation scenarios and lock-in}

We recompute energy under each measure of the national strategy, alone and in combination (Methods; Fig.~\ref{fig:scenarios}). None of the measures acts on the form of the neighbourhood, so access remains as measured. Every scenario reduces absolute energy consumption in every neighbourhood, but the three measures act differently on the gap between forms. Fully deployed insulation closes more of the gap than any other single measure, moving it from \nepiscenarioBase$\times$ to \nepifabricGap$\times$. Vehicle electrification closes nearly as much, leaving \nepievGap$\times$. Heat pumps cut home energy sharply but near-uniformly, which raises the relative weight of the larger travel gap and leaves the total marginally wider, at \nepihpGap$\times$. The Climate Change Committee's Balanced Pathway for 2040, advised for the legislated carbon budgets, sets half of homes on heat pumps and three-quarters of cars electric \citep{ccc2025}, which brings the gap to \nepicccGap$\times$ (per-scenario confidence intervals in Extended Data Table~2).

The lock-in is read at full deployment of all three measures: the total gap falls from \nepiscenarioBase$\times$ to \nepifullGap$\times$ (\nepifullGapCI), so \nepifullSurvives\% of it survives. Part of that gap exists because detached homes hold larger households, so we repeat the exercise with household size held equal: full deployment carries the gap from \nepifamNowGap$\times$ to \nepifullFamGap$\times$ (\nepifullFamGapCI). The premium remains notable by the introduction's bar. Neighbourhoods of the same composition, age, tenure, and climate still differ among themselves even when fully treated: the middle half of like areas spans a factor of \nepiwithinSpread. The surviving gap of \nepifullGap$\times$ is wider than that whole band, separating the types by \nepifullGapSd{} within-type standard deviations, so the typical treated detached-type area lies beyond the middle range of treated flat-type ones. The band narrows to \nepiwithinSpreadFam{} when household sizes are held equal, in which case the gap still sits outside it (Methods).

\subsection*{Robustness}

Who lives where could, in principle, do the work we attribute to form: if the households that choose detached houses are simply heavier energy users, the gap would describe them and not their neighbourhoods. Such households may live and spend in ways that the controls---building age, income deprivation, tenure, and climate---do not record. We check this in two ways. Adding occupational class, a strong marker of who lives where, changes nothing meaningful. And the measured controls barely moved the gap when they entered, so an unrecorded difference would have to be as large as everything we already control for to remove the total gap (coefficient-stability bound $\delta^{*} \approx \nepiosterDeltaTotal$ \citep{oster2019}). The home-energy gap alone would be more sensitive, but the claims rest on the total, in which travel is the larger part (Extended Data Table~3).

The travel gap could come from our own arithmetic, because no area's driving is observed directly: the survey measures each settlement class's mileage, and we distribute it across the class's areas by a rule. The travel gap stays at or above \nepialphaZeroGap$\times$ (\nepialphaZeroGapCI), down from the headline \nepitravelGap$\times$, even when that rule is compressed to its flattest limit and every area in a class is assigned the same per-person mileage. Charging public-transport energy to residents on the same construction leaves the total gap at \nepiptAssumedTotal$\times$ (\nepiptAssumedTotalCI), down from \nepitotalGap$\times$ (Extended Data Tables~5 and~6).

Metering could bias the home-energy gap. Flat-type areas hold fewer domestic gas meters than households, which leaves some flat energy uncounted. Restricting the sample to areas whose meters cover their households raises the gap slightly, so the missing meters understate it (Extended Data Table~6).

Re-fitting the same model at the two coarser census geographies moves the total gap from \nepitotalGap$\times$ at Output Area scale to \nepimaupTotalLsoa$\times$ one level up and \nepimaupTotalMsoa$\times$ two levels up, so the result does not depend on the size of the areas compared, although the home-energy contrast alone attenuates at the coarsest scale (Extended Data Table~4). Merging the districts into fifty contiguous spatial blocks widens the total-gap interval only slightly and leaves the estimate unchanged, so clustering on districts is sufficient (Methods). Figure~\ref{fig:forest} collects every headline ratio with its clustered confidence interval.

\section*{Discussion}

Measures applied to individual homes and vehicles do not cancel the energy premium of dispersed urban form. A detached-type neighbourhood uses \nepitotalGap$\times$ the energy of a flat-type one today; full deployment of insulation, heat pumps, and electric vehicles leaves \nepifullGap$\times$; and the on-foot access gap of \nepiwalkAmenRound$\times$ is untouched in every scenario, because no measure moves a destination. The premium remains notable by the bar set at the outset, and it survives because the measures change how efficiently energy is used rather than how much heating and driving the form requires. Insulation lowers the heat lost per square metre but leaves the floor area unchanged, electrification lowers the energy per mile but leaves the mileage unchanged, and a heat pump delivers the same heat with less energy but moves nothing closer. The floor area and the distances are set in built form and street layout for generations. This is lock-in in the strict sense \citep{unruh2000,seto2016}. A fully renewable grid would lower the emissions of the premium without narrowing it, because form fixes the demand while the carbon content of a kilowatt-hour is a property of the supply.

Full deployment leaves a flat-type neighbourhood running on about \nepifullFlatKwh{} kilowatt-hours per dwelling a year for homes and cars together, and a detached-type neighbourhood on about \nepifullDetKwh, which is the whole flat-type budget and two-thirds of it again. That difference, summed across the existing stock, comes to about \nepistockPremiumTwh{} terawatt-hours a year, roughly a fifth of what the fully treated stock would use, or \nepistockFamPremiumTwh{} once the part explained by larger households is set aside. The existing stock will not be rebuilt, so that figure stands as a running cost of the form already built. The form of the stock added each year is still to be decided: every 100,000 dwellings built as detached-type rather than flat-type neighbourhoods commit a further \nepifullPremiumHundredK{} terawatt-hours a year for as long as they stand.

The metered home-energy gap of \nepiheatGap$\times$ agrees with the national consumption statistics and metered regressions \citep{need2024,ehs2024,wyatt2013}. It narrows and slightly reverses per square metre (\nepimSqGap$\times$). On the travel side, the amenities reachable at each type's own car catchment come out nearly equal (\nepicatchAmen$\times$); we take the balance as some assurance that the allocated mileage and the amenity basket describe residents' actual needs.

The policy instruments in current use cover only part of the result. Certificates, fabric subsidy, and boiler replacement all act on home energy, whose gap between forms is the smaller of the two (Table~\ref{tab:axes}); the larger travel gap has no dedicated instrument, because it is generated by the neighbourhood's location rather than by an asset a household owns. No certificate, subsidy, or planning metric records the largest differential, the \nepirate$\times$ access per kilowatt-hour of driving, so building compact, which improves both quantities at once, is absent from the accounting. Scoring access alongside energy addresses both omissions, and the rate expresses proximity in the same unit as retrofit and electrification. We refer to the two quantities and the rate between them as the neighbourhood energy performance index (NEPI).

Revaluing access has a distributional consequence. Access is ordinarily capitalised into land prices and rents \citep{alonso1964}, but in England today the distribution runs the other way: the median area in the most income-deprived decile reaches about \nepiimdAccessRatio$\times$ the walkable amenities of the least deprived decile, and the least deprived decile uses \nepiimdEnergyRatio$\times$ the energy of the most deprived, because deprivation is concentrated in compact form and affluence in dispersed form. The pattern already inverts where proximity carries a price: the access-deprivation correlation is positive across England but near zero or negative in inner London, Manchester, and Bristol (Extended Data Fig.~4). Revaluing proximity without expanding its supply would repeat that inversion everywhere, pricing lower-income households out of the neighbourhoods the decarbonisation case favours; planning for access therefore means expanding the supply of compact neighbourhoods as well as scoring the existing stock.

Travel energy counts car travel only; charging public-transport energy to residents leaves the total gap little changed (Extended Data Table~5). The analysis is ecological \citep{robinson1950,greenland2001}; the definitive self-selection test, following movers in panel microdata, is out of scope. The insulation scenario assumes the certificate potential-to-current ratio captures achievable fabric improvement. Energy saved through efficiency is partly offset by rebound \citep{sorrell2007}, so dwelling-level gains need not scale to economy-wide reductions. The analysis covers England only.

The energy productivity principle's prediction, that compact form obtains more use from an equivalent unit of energy, is confirmed at neighbourhood scale, and its size is measured. In the terms of the opening illustration, the dispersed neighbourhood is the desert, where an equivalent unit of energy streams through and yields a single use; the compact neighbourhood extracts \nepirate$\times$ the use from the same unit. These figures cover housing and access to land uses, the two uses these datasets measure, and a fuller account of the complexity Jacobs described would likely place the advantage of compact form higher still. The premium is fixed at the point of construction and persists until places are rebuilt. Decarbonisation strategy should therefore place greater emphasis on compact urban form, and on the mixed-use development with which planning practice pairs it, though only the first of those is measured here.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Unit of analysis}

The unit is the 2021 Census Output Area, the smallest published census geography, with about 125 households and 300 residents. Of England's 178,605 Output Areas, the \nepisampleN{} with usable metered energy (areas with more than ten residents and positive metered consumption) are analysed. Wales is excluded because the English Index of Multiple Deprivation, used as a control, has no directly comparable Welsh equivalent. All inputs are open data, processed to the Output Area on the British National Grid (EPSG:27700): Census 2021 topic tables (population, household size, dwelling-type shares, tenure, occupational class, car ownership, commute distance, workplace jobs); DESNZ sub-national gas and electricity statistics; domestic Energy Performance Certificates (floor area, build year, current and potential intensity, aggregated as area medians); OS Open Roads; amenity registers (Food Standards Agency, Get Information about Schools, NHS Organisation Data Service, OS Open Greenspace); the Indices of Deprivation 2025; DVLA vehicle licensing; National Travel Survey table NTS9904; and HadUK-Grid 1991--2020 heating-degree-days.

\subsection*{Energy}

Energy is delivered energy in kilowatt-hours per dwelling per year, not primary energy or carbon. The home component is metered rather than modelled. Postcode-level gas and electricity, each supplied as a meter count and a mean per meter, are aggregated to the Output Area by meter-weighted means, summed, and divided by census households. Gas is weather-corrected. Electricity is separated by meter profile class: the domestic sector comprises the non-half-hourly standard and Economy 7 profiles, with meters on those profiles reallocated to the commercial sector above 100,000 kWh per year. Gas has no use-based flag; every meter consuming less than 73,200 kWh per year is classified as domestic, so small commercial premises remain in the domestic series. Such premises are concentrated in the compact areas that hold ground-floor commercial stock, and their consumption enters the numerator without adding to the household denominator, so the leakage acts against the reported form gradient.

The travel component is the total car-travel energy of a location's residents, not the commute alone (about one sixth of car mileage). No area's driving is observed directly, so the travel component is estimated by constrained disaggregation. NTS9904 gives measured car-driver miles per person by 2021 rural-urban class of residence, from about 2,500 miles per person per year (urban) to 5,200 (rural). Only the distribution within each class is estimated, and each class's population-weighted mean is constrained to reproduce the survey figure exactly. Within a class, mileage is allocated by cars per person (elasticity 1, the proportional headline) and commute distance (elasticity 0.30), and both elasticities are swept in the sensitivity analysis. Miles are converted to energy at the local fleet intensity, \nepitravelIce{} kWh per mile (internal combustion) and \nepitravelEv{} (battery electric), blended by the DVLA battery-electric share. The intensities are consistent with the Department for Transport's energy-consumption and appraisal tables. Allocated per-person miles are multiplied by census household size to give miles per dwelling before conversion to energy, so household size is part of the per-dwelling travel contrast. Commute distance enters the allocator as the household-weighted mean of the census distance-band midpoints. Metered domestic electricity already includes home electric-vehicle charging, so the travel figure double-counts that overlap; at the current battery-electric fleet share (2--3\%) the effect is negligible. Public-transport mileage from the same survey table is disaggregated in the same way for the sensitivity reported in Extended Data Table~5, with the Census TS061 public-transport share of commuters as the within-class allocator; it does not enter the headline energy total.

\subsection*{Access}

Access is the count of opportunities reachable from an Output Area over the road network. The England network (OS Open Roads, about 3.6 million junctions) is built once into a routable graph (cityseer \citep{simons2023}), and counts are accumulated outwards from each Output Area's nearest node at every distance band from 1,600\,m to 25,600\,m. Amenities are a benchmark basket of six everyday destination types, counted identically for every Output Area: general practitioners and pharmacies (NHS), schools (GIAS), places to eat and drink and grocery shops (FSA), and public parks and greenspace (Ordnance Survey); sources, record counts, and filters are given in Extended Data Table~1. Jobs are workplace jobs, each workplace weighted by its job count; people are the resident population. Each is read at 1,600\,m, at the area's own car catchment, and at 25,600\,m.
\subsection*{The rate}

For each Output Area, the rate is the amenity count within the area's own car catchment divided by its car-travel energy. The catchment is the area's allocated annual car-driver distance per person divided by 370 trips per person per year, approximating the National Travel Survey car-driver trip rate (table NTS0303: 363 in 2023, about 380 before the pandemic). The resulting one-way distance is used as the network reach, bounded to [1,600\,m, 25,600\,m]. Recomputing the catchments at 300 and 440 trips per year moves the rate only from \nepitripMidRate$\times$ to \nepitripLowRate$\times$ and \nepitripHighRate$\times$, so the level of the constant has little effect. The flat-to-detached contrast of the rate is by construction the product of the access advantage (catchment amenities, flat:detached) and the energy saving (car-travel energy, detached:flat); it is reconstructable from Table~\ref{tab:axes} rather than estimated as a separate model. The catchment radius and the travel energy are both functions of how far residents drive; two variants bound the rate's dependence on that construction. One recomputes the denominator as total energy, home plus travel at electric fleet intensity, so that driving distance is only a small share of it, and gives \nepirateCirc$\times$ (\nepirateCircCI). The other re-estimates the energy ratio holding the income domain alone, matching the conditioning of the access ratio, and gives \nepirateHarm$\times$ (\nepirateHarmCI).

\subsection*{The compositional model}

All contrasts derive from one compositional, no-intercept regression on each Output Area's dwelling-type shares (flat, terraced, semi-detached, detached, residual) as fractions summing to one. Without an intercept, each coefficient is the modelled outcome of a neighbourhood composed entirely of one type, and the ratio is $\exp(b_{\mathrm{detached}} - b_{\mathrm{flat}})$; the confounds enter uncentred and shift both pure-type predictions equally, so the ratio does not depend on the levels at which they are held. Regressions are household-weighted. The median of dominant types is reported alongside: the ratio of medians between areas whose plurality dwelling type is flat and those whose plurality type is detached.

Energy enters in logarithms per dwelling, so fitted ratios are ratios of conditional geometric means. Household size and floor area are covariates with freely estimated coefficients, not denominators. The bivariate floor-area elasticity of home energy is \nepifloorElast{} and the within-model household-size elasticity is \nepihhElast, both well below one. Four confounds are held throughout: building age, deprivation (the overall Index of Multiple Deprivation and its income domain), tenure (social- and private-rented shares), and climate (heating-degree-days). Access counts are non-negative with frequent zeros and are fitted with a Poisson log link; with households as analytic weights the estimator is quasi-Poisson, using point estimates with a robust sandwich covariance. The access regression holds the income domain; it excludes density, which is the mechanism under study, and the overall deprivation index, whose barriers and living-environment sub-domains are themselves access measures.

Standard errors are clustered on the \nepiclusters{} local-authority districts, with $t(G-1)$ critical values; every reported ratio carries a 95\% interval on this basis. To check that districts are large enough to contain the spatial dependence, the total-gap interval was re-estimated with the districts merged into fifty contiguous spatial blocks (k-means on district centroids); it widens to \nepispatialBlockGapCI with the estimate unchanged. Composite quantities (the rate and the insulation-plus-electrification surviving-gap share) carry cluster-bootstrap intervals with 999 replications, resampling whole districts; the per-scenario ratios carry their own clustered intervals (Extended Data Table~2), from which the scenario closure shares derive.

\subsection*{Decarbonisation scenarios}

Energy is recomputed under the technology measures and the gap re-estimated. Insulation scales each home's metered gas by its certificate fabric-improvement ratio (modelled potential over current intensity, median \nepifabricFactorMedian, clipped to the unit interval; both terms are modelled, so the performance gap cancels); electricity is unchanged. Heat pumps deliver the gas heat as electricity at the delivered-energy factor of boiler efficiency over a seasonal coefficient of performance ($0.90/2.8 = 0.32$); a sensitivity analysis varies the coefficient between 2.4 and 3.2. Electrification recomputes car energy at electric fleet intensity with mileage unchanged. Both heat measures act on metered gas only, so electrically heated homes, more common among flats, receive no retrofit measure; this bias is conservative for the surviving-gap shares. Partial uptake blends transformed and untransformed energy linearly; the Balanced Pathway scenario uses the Climate Change Committee's 2040 stock shares \citep{ccc2025}. Every scenario is fitted on one common complete-case sample (\nepiscenarioN{} areas with a valid fabric ratio). Access is unchanged in every scenario, since no measure moves a destination. The sufficiency bar compares the surviving gap with the band of variation among like neighbourhoods. We take the residuals of the same fit that produces the gap, in which composition and the confounds are already held, and report the band as the factor between the 25th and 75th percentiles of those residuals; the gap is also expressed in residual standard deviations. The residuals carry the noise of allocating postcode meters and survey mileage to areas, so the band overstates the real variation between like places and the bar errs in favour of the measures. The absolute premium is the difference between the two pure-type predictions at household-weighted mean confounds. The stock-wide figure re-prices every area at wholly flat composition, keeping its own confounds, household size, and residual, and sums the difference over households; it prices the standing cost of dispersed form, and implies no rebuilding.

\subsection*{Robustness and the estimand}

The estimand is place-level: the energy and access profile of a neighbourhood type conditional on observed confounds, not a household treatment effect. Three checks bound selection on unobservables: access depends on location alone and is therefore unaffected by sorting. Occupational class (higher managerial and professional share) is added to the confounds and leaves the gaps essentially unchanged. A coefficient-stability bound \citep{oster2019} is computed on a continuous detached-share gradient in an intercept specification (the compositional model has no centred $R^2$), using the coefficient-based approximation with $R_{\max} = \min(1.3\tilde{R}, 1)$; the Results report $\delta^{*}$, and Extended Data Table~3 the $\delta = 1$ bias-adjusted gap. The zonation check re-aggregates all variables, household-weighted, to LSOA and MSOA geographies and re-fits the identical model.
